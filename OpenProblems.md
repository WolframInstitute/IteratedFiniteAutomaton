# Open Problems

Problems in group theory, each paired with the statement about a finite automaton that would settle it.

The [README table](README.md#-open-problems) is the short version: which conjectures an automaton group has already decided.
This is the long version, and it is written to be *used* — every entry says what a witness would look like, which paclet call searches for one, and how far this repository has already searched.

Throughout $G$ is a finitely generated group, $K$ is a field, $T_k$ is the rooted $k$-ary tree, and an *automaton* is a finite invertible Mealy automaton over a $k$-letter alphabet whose states generate a subgroup of $\mathrm{Aut}(T_k)$.
Citations link into [`References.md`](References.md), one anchor per key.

## How to read an entry

Every entry carries the same eight fields, so the catalog is a table and not an essay.
Two of them take values from a fixed list, which is the point: a translation that is only an analogy has to say so in a field, where it cannot be buried in prose.

**Status** is one of `Open`, `Solved by an automaton group`, `Solved by other means`, `Partially solved`, `Undecidable` — followed by the year and who.

**Faithfulness** is one of:

| Value | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `Equivalent` | the automaton statement holds if and only if the group statement does |
| `Sufficient` | a witness on the automaton side settles the group problem; **absence of one proves nothing** |
| `Necessary` | implied by the group statement, so it is usable as a search filter and nothing more |
| `Heuristic` | an analogy — no theorem connects the two sides |
| `None` | no translation into automaton terms is known; the entry is here so the gap is visible |

The remaining six are free text: **Problem** (the group-theoretic statement), **Automaton translation**, **Witness shape** (states, alphabet size, support radius, level depth), **Test recipe** (the paclet call), **Searched here** (what this repository has ruled out, with its bound), and **References**.

`Searched here` is a claim about *this repository's* computations only, always with the bound that makes it falsifiable.
An empty search is recorded as an empty search, never as evidence.

## Group rings

### <a id="KaplanskyZeroDivisor"></a>Kaplansky zero-divisor conjecture

| Field | |
|---|---|
| **Problem** | For $G$ torsion-free, $K[G]$ is a domain: if $A, B \in K[G]$ are nonzero then $AB \neq 0$. Stated in Kaplansky's 1970 problem list, which is where the name comes from; the conjectures go back to Higman's 1940 thesis. Torsion-freeness is necessary, not incidental — if $g^n = 1$ then $(1 - g)(1 + g + \cdots + g^{n-1}) = 0$. |
| **Status** | `Open` (since the 1950s). Known for left-orderable $G$ — Higman 1940, reached in practice through local indicability, Burns–Hale 1972 — and for torsion-free elementary amenable $G$, Kropholler–Linnell–Moody 1988. The implications run unit $\Rightarrow$ zero-divisor $\Rightarrow$ idempotent, so Gardam's 2021 refutation of the unit conjecture leaves this one untouched. |
| **Automaton translation** | Fix an invertible Mealy automaton on $s$ states over $k$ letters whose group $G$ is torsion-free, and a radius $r$; write $S_r$ for the radius-$r$ ball in $G$ and $G_L$ for the image of $G$ in its action on the $k^L$ vertices of level $L$. Each $G_L$ is finite, $G_{L+1} \twoheadrightarrow G_L$, and the actions are faithful in the limit, so a product of two elements supported on $S_r$ vanishes in $K[G]$ **iff** it vanishes in $K[G_L]$ for every $L$. A zero divisor supported on $S_r$ is therefore exactly a pair of nonzero coefficient vectors $c, d \in \mathbb{F}_p^{S_r}$ surviving the whole level tower. Two products $s_i s_j$ equal at level $L+1$ are equal at level $L$, so each row of the multiplication matrix $M_L(c)$ is a sum of rows of $M_{L+1}(c)$ and the kernels nest, $\ker M_{L+1}(c) \subseteq \ker M_L(c)$: one empty level is a proof for that radius, field and weight bound. |
| **Faithfulness** | `Equivalent` — for a fixed $G$ and a fixed support ball, by the residual finiteness above, and taking the union over all radii gives the conjecture for $G$ exactly. Two scope limits, neither of them elidable. A search that stops at a finite level has only ever *refuted*: the survivor sets are nested subspaces of a fixed $\mathbb{F}_p^n$ so the chain must stabilise, but finitely many equal terms never show that it has, and a chain can plateau and then drop. And automaton groups are a proper subclass of the torsion-free groups, so exhausting a space of automata bounds nothing about the conjecture itself. |
| **Witness shape** | An invertible Mealy automaton, $s$ states over $k$ letters, generating a torsion-free group; two nonzero coefficient vectors over the radius-$r$ ball. Radius-1 supports run to 5 elements in $(2, 3)$, 7 in $(3, 2)$ and 9 in $(4, 2)$; radius 2 reaches 17, 37 and 65 respectively. The smallest instance a reader can check by hand is a 3-element support at level 3: quotient of order 8, nine products on five values, seven nonzero $\mathbb{F}_2$-vectors, a $5 \times 3$ matrix each. A *negative* certificate has the same shape — one left inverse of $M_L(c)$ per nonzero $c$, which is a $\forall$ over a finite type with a decidable body and so discharged by `decide` in Lean. |
| **Test recipe** | `GroupRingZeroDivisorSurvival[automaton, radius, levels, Modulus -> p]` is the search, and it is the call all four sweeps in `Searched here` were built from. It returns `<|"Code", "Support", "Counts", "Survivors", "Annihilators"|>`: `"Counts"` records how many candidates remain after each level, an empty `"Survivors"` **is** the refutation for that radius and field, and every survivor comes paired with a witness $B$ satisfying $AB = 0$ at each tested level. `MaxTerms -> t` imposes the weight bound and `MaxTerms -> Infinity` makes the pass exhaustive over the ball, which is the difference between the $(2, 3)$ radius-2 result and every other radius-2 result quoted below; `Method -> "Annihilator"` swaps the level-by-level filter for one nullspace per candidate. Verified: `GroupRingZeroDivisorSurvival[{5125, {3, 2}}, 1, {3, 5, 7}, Modulus -> 2]` reports a 7-word support and `"Counts"` $\{\{3, 5\}, \{5, 0\}\}$ — five candidates survive level 3, none survives level 5, so the Aleshin class is refuted at radius 1 over $\mathbb{F}_2$ and the pass stops without reaching level 7. The positive control is `{4210, {3, 2}}`, the lamplighter padded with a trivial state and therefore *not* torsion-free: it keeps 2 survivors through level 7 with their annihilators, as it must. Single instances: `GroupRingLeftAnnihilator[automaton, a, support, levels, Modulus -> p]` for one fixed $A$, and `GroupRingLevelProduct[automaton, level, a, b]`, which is `<||>` exactly when $AB = 0$ in $G_L$. Group-ring elements are lists of `{coefficient, word}` pairs — `{}` is $0$, `{{1, {}}}` is $1$ — supports come from `AutomatonGroupBall[automaton, radius, refLevel]`, and the torsion-free filter feeding the whole pipeline is `AutomatonTorsionFreeCandidateQ[automaton, wordLength, levels]`. |
| **Searched here** | Every torsion-free candidate in four spaces is refuted — 6018 groups in total — but always with both factors confined to the ball, which is the bound that makes the claim falsifiable. $(2, 2)$: of 64 invertible rules, 16 generate the trivial group and 8 are torsion-free candidates (2 classes, both $\cong \mathbb{Z}$); all 24 refuted at radius 1 over $\mathbb{F}_2$ through level 8, while the remaining 40 — which have torsion — produce a surviving pair, as they must. $(3, 2)$: 5832 invertible automata funnel to 133 distinct torsion-free candidates, all refuted; radius 1 exhaustive over $\mathbb{F}_2, \mathbb{F}_3, \mathbb{F}_5, \mathbb{F}_7$ (supports never exceed 7) and weight $\leq 4$ over $\mathbb{F}_{11}, \mathbb{F}_{13}$, deepest refutation level 7 against a ceiling of 12; radius 2 weight $\leq 2$ over $\mathbb{F}_5, \mathbb{F}_7$ plus weight $\leq 3$ over $\mathbb{F}_5$ on the 32 intermediate groups, zero by level 9 against a ceiling of 10. $(2, 3)$: all 55 candidates refuted, radius 1 exhaustive over six primes (level 3 for 49 of them, level 4 for 6), and radius 2 exhaustive over $\mathbb{F}_2$ — support 17, so all $2^{17} - 1$ coefficient vectors — which is the only exhaustive radius-2 pass in the project. $(4, 2)$: all 5822 candidates refuted, radius 1 exhaustive over $\mathbb{F}_2$ and weight-bounded elsewhere, deepest level 9 against a ceiling of 10, so one level of margin where $(3, 2)$ had two to five. **The standing gap is radius 2 in $(3, 2)$ and $(4, 2)$, which is weight-bounded and therefore not exhaustive.** What an empty pass proves, stated exactly: there are no nonzero $A, B \in \mathbb{F}_p[G]$ with supports in the radius-$r$ ball and $AB = 0$ — a theorem about the ball, not about $\mathbb{F}_p[G]$. Of the 32 informative $(3, 2)$ codes, 10 fall in three classes ($BS(1, 3)$, $BS(1, -3)$, the Klein bottle group) that are left-orderable and solvable, so for those the empty search is a consistency check rather than evidence. The sharpest genuinely open case swept is the **Basilica group**, torsion-free and amenable but not even subexponentially amenable, hence outside both known theorems. |
| **References** | **Problem** [Kaplansky1970](References.md#Kaplansky1970), with the conjectures traced to [Higman1940](References.md#Higman1940); [Passman1977](References.md#Passman1977) for the group-ring background. **Known cases** [Higman1940](References.md#Higman1940) and [BurnsHale1972](References.md#BurnsHale1972) (left-orderable, via local indicability), [KLM1988](References.md#KLM1988) (torsion-free elementary amenable), [Chou1980](References.md#Chou1980) for what that class contains. **Implications** [Gardam2021](References.md#Gardam2021) refutes the unit conjecture without touching this one. **Automaton side** [GNS2000](References.md#GNS2000) and [Nekrashevych2005](References.md#Nekrashevych2005) for residual finiteness and the level tower. **The groups swept** [GrigorchukZuk2002](References.md#GrigorchukZuk2002) (Basilica, the sharpest open case, outside both known theorems by [BartholdiVirag2005](References.md#BartholdiVirag2005)), [BGKMNSS2007a](References.md#BGKMNSS2007a) and [BGKMNSS2009](References.md#BGKMNSS2009) for the $(3, 2)$ classification the funnel reports against. |

### <a id="KaplanskyIdempotent"></a>Kaplansky idempotent conjecture

| Field | |
|---|---|
| **Problem** | For $G$ torsion-free, $K[G]$ has no idempotents besides $0$ and $1$. |
| **Status** | `Open` (since the 1950s). The weakest of the three: a nontrivial idempotent $e$ gives the zero divisor $e(1 - e) = 0$, so the zero-divisor conjecture implies this one, and the unit conjecture implies that. Known for the same classes — left-orderable, and torsion-free elementary amenable. |
| **Automaton translation** | The zero-divisor translation with the second factor forced: a nonzero $A$ supported on the radius-$r$ ball with $A(A - 1) = 0$, tested against the same level tower and refuted by the same nesting. |
| **Faithfulness** | `Equivalent` — same residual-finiteness argument, same two scope limits as the zero-divisor entry. Strictly weaker as a *search*, though: an idempotent is a special zero divisor, so an empty idempotent pass over a given ball and field is already implied by an empty zero-divisor pass over the same ball and field, and adds nothing to it. |
| **Witness shape** | One nonzero coefficient vector rather than two, over the same balls as above. Characteristic 2 is *not* a general shortcut here — the identity $\left(\sum_i g_i\right)^2 = \sum_i g_i^2$ requires the support to commute, and fails as soon as it does not, e.g. $(1 + x + y)^2 = 1 + x^2 + y^2 + xy + yx$ in $\mathbb{F}_2[F_2]$. It does settle the abelian case outright: if $\langle \operatorname{supp} A \rangle$ is abelian and torsion-free then $A^2 = A$ forces squaring to permute the support, so $g^{2^n} = g$ for some $n$ and every $g$ in it is trivial. So over $\mathbb{F}_2$ the test is vacuous exactly on the abelian stratum and is a genuine test off it. What a positive witness looks like, from the torsion control: for an involution $g$, the element $c(1 + g)$ is idempotent precisely when $2c^2 = c$, i.e. $c = (p+1)/2$ — one solution for every odd $p$, none in characteristic 2. |
| **Test recipe** | `GroupRingIdempotentSurvival[automaton, radius, levels, Modulus -> p]` — the same tower filter with the second factor forced, the same `<|"Code", "Support", "Counts", "Survivors"|>` shape and the same `MaxTerms -> t` weight bound. Verified: `GroupRingIdempotentSurvival[{5125, {3, 2}}, 1, {3, 5, 7}, Modulus -> 3]` gives `"Counts"` $\{\{3, 0\}\}$, so nothing survives the first level tested. For an exhaustive enumeration inside a *single* quotient rather than along the tower, `GroupRingIdempotentSearch[automaton, radius, coefficients, level]` returns the idempotents themselves with $0$ and $1$ excluded — the call that produced the 16 $\mathbb{F}_3$ idempotents of the Grigorchuk control — and one candidate is checked with `GroupRingIdempotentQ[automaton, a, level]`. Two economies follow from the field text rather than from the code. An idempotent is a special zero divisor, so an empty `GroupRingZeroDivisorSurvival` over the same ball and field already implies an empty pass here; run this one only where the zero-divisor pass is out of reach at that radius. And over $\mathbb{F}_2$ the pass is vacuous on the abelian stratum, which `AutomatonAbelianQ[automaton, level]` separates out, so `Modulus -> 2` is worth spending only on the codes it reports `False`. |
| **Searched here** | $(3, 2)$ only, all 133 torsion-free candidates: radius 1 in full over $\mathbb{F}_2$ and $\mathbb{F}_3$, radius 2 weight-bounded over $\mathbb{F}_2$ and $\mathbb{F}_3$, all through level 9 — **no nontrivial idempotent in any group**. On the 36 abelian groups the $\mathbb{F}_2$ half of that is forced by the argument above and carries no information; the content is the 97 non-abelian groups over $\mathbb{F}_2$ and all 133 over $\mathbb{F}_3$. Validated in both directions: empty on $\mathbb{Z}$ (the adding machine, code 62), and on the torsion Grigorchuk group it returns nothing over $\mathbb{F}_2$ but 16 idempotents over $\mathbb{F}_3$, the smallest being the $c(1 + g)$ above. Nothing searched in $(2, 2)$, $(2, 3)$ or $(4, 2)$, where only the zero-divisor sweep ran — though those sweeps subsume the corresponding idempotent question at the radii and fields they covered. |
| **References** | **Problem and known cases** as for the [zero divisor entry](#KaplanskyZeroDivisor) — [Kaplansky1970](References.md#Kaplansky1970), [Higman1940](References.md#Higman1940), [BurnsHale1972](References.md#BurnsHale1972), [KLM1988](References.md#KLM1988), [Passman1977](References.md#Passman1977). **The characteristic-2 correction** is elementary and is worked out in this repository rather than cited: the freshman's-dream identity needs a commuting support, and the abelian case is what survives. **Controls** [Grigorchuk1980](References.md#Grigorchuk1980) for the torsion group whose $\mathbb{F}_3$ idempotents validate the search, [GrigorchukZuk2001](References.md#GrigorchukZuk2001) for the adding-machine/lamplighter pair the abelian side is checked on. |

### <a id="KaplanskyUnit"></a>Kaplansky unit conjecture

| Field | |
|---|---|
| **Problem** | For $G$ torsion-free and $K$ a field, every unit of $K[G]$ is trivial, i.e. of the form $\lambda g$ with $\lambda \in K^\times$ and $g \in G$. |
| **Status** | `Solved by other means` — **false**, Gardam 2021, by an explicit unit in $\mathbb{F}_2[P]$ with $P$ the Promislow (Hantzsche–Wendt) group, found by SAT search and formalized against Mathlib by Gadgil–Tadipatri 2024. It is the strongest of the three, so its failure says nothing about the zero-divisor or idempotent conjectures. Gardam 2023 then refuted it in **characteristic zero** on the same 21-element support, hence also over $\mathbb{F}_{p^2}$ for every prime $p$; over $\mathbb{Z}$ it is still open. |
| **Automaton translation** | Two, of different strength, and *(i) is now carried out.* **(i) Realize the known counterexample.** $P = \langle a, b \mid b^{-1} a^2 b = a^{-2},\ a^{-1} b^2 a = b^{-2} \rangle$ is torsion-free and virtually $\mathbb{Z}^3$, and it **is** an automaton group: 25 states on 3 letters, from the virtual endomorphism that conjugates by $\operatorname{diag}(1/3, 1, 1)$ times the cyclic shift of the coordinate axes, defined on $H = \{g : 3 \mid v_3(g)\}$ of index 3. It is finite-state because the spectral radius is $3^{-1/3} < 1$ and faithful because the same contraction makes the $\varphi$-core trivial, and **degree 3 is least**: writing the diagonal of the monomial linear part as $p_j / q_j$ in lowest terms gives $[L : T] = q_1 q_2 q_3$, all $q_j$ are odd once all four point parts survive, an integral monomial matrix is not contracting, and a proper point group costs a factor $\ge 2$ on top of $[L : T] \ge 2$ — so degree 2 is impossible from either side. So Gardam's units, in both characteristics, are counterexamples to the unit conjecture **inside the class of automaton groups**. **(ii) Search directly.** For a torsion-free automaton group $G$, look for $u, v$ supported on the radius-$r$ ball with $uv = 1$ and $u$ not of the form $\lambda g$. Refutation by the level tower still works, since a relation in $G$ pushes forward to every $G_L$ and the level maps are surjections; but the solution set is no longer a linear subspace, so the sweep's inner test changes — for fixed $u$ it becomes the inhomogeneous solve $M_L(c)\, d = e_1$, decided by a rank comparison rather than by a kernel. |
| **Faithfulness** | `Sufficient`. The conjecture is already answered, so no automaton witness decides it; what route (i) supplies is a counterexample inside a self-similar group, which is now available. Absence still proves nothing in either direction, since a finite sweep bounds only the ball. |
| **Witness shape** | Gardam's is the template. With $x = a^2$, $y = b^2$, $z = (ab)^2$ generating a $\mathbb{Z}^3$ of index 4 in $P$, the unit is $p + qa + rb + sab$ where $p = (1 + x)(1 + y)(1 + z^{-1})$, $q = x^{-1} y^{-1} + x + y^{-1} z + z$, $r = 1 + x + y^{-1} z + xyz$, $s = 1 + (x + x^{-1} + y + y^{-1}) z^{-1}$ — 21 group elements in the support once expanded. That sets the scale for the automaton side: radius 1 in every space swept here holds at most 9 elements and is far too small, and radius 2 (17 to 65 elements) is the first plausible range. |
| **Test recipe** | **No search exists**, and this is the one entry whose gap is a missing function rather than a missing idea. The paclet has `GroupRingZeroDivisorSurvival` and `GroupRingIdempotentSurvival` and no $AB = 1$ sweep, because both existing searches filter nullspaces and the unit condition is an inhomogeneous solve. The *check* does exist, which is enough to verify a candidate produced elsewhere: $uv = 1$ in $G_L$ iff `GroupRingLevelProduct[automaton, level, u, v]` is the single-entry association `<|Range[k^level] -> 1|>`, the identity permutation with coefficient $1$. Verified on the trivial unit of the adding machine — `GroupRingLevelProduct[{62, {2, 2}}, 3, {{1, {1}}}, {{1, {-1}}}]` returns `<|{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8} -> 1|>`. Writing the search is one function and no new mathematics: for fixed $u$ supported on the ball it is the solve $M_L(c)\, d = e_1$ against the same $0/1$ left-multiplication matrices `Method -> "Tower"` already precomputes, decided by a rank comparison. Left out deliberately, not overlooked — the conjecture is already settled, so the payoff is a self-similar counterexample rather than an answer, and route (i) supplies one without any search at all. |
| **Searched here** | **No sweep, one witness.** The paclet's group-ring code tests $AB = 0$ and $A(A - 1) = 0$ and has no $AB = 1$ search, so there is still no searched bound to report. What exists instead is route (i) carried out: the 25-state degree-3 automaton for $P$, with Gardam 2023's Theorem A reverified both in the affine model of $P$ and with every group element replaced by its permutation of the $3^6 = 729$ vertices of level 6, where the 441 products of the two 21-element supports first separate into their 121 distinct values — the paper's 121 equations; the counts run $95, 101, 111, 121$ from level 3. A *direct* search still needs radius 2, where every pass in this repository outside $(2, 3)$ has had to accept a weight bound. |
| **References** | **Refutation** [Gardam2021](References.md#Gardam2021) over $\mathbb{F}_2$, formalized against Mathlib by [GadgilTadipatri2024](References.md#GadgilTadipatri2024) over [MathlibMonoidAlgebra](References.md#MathlibMonoidAlgebra) — see also [MathlibCommunity2020](References.md#MathlibCommunity2020) and [MouraUllrich2021](References.md#MouraUllrich2021) for the system it is formalized in — and [Gardam2023](References.md#Gardam2023) in characteristic zero. **The group** [Promislow1988](References.md#Promislow1988) for $P$ and its torsion-freeness. **The realization** rests on the virtual-endomorphism correspondence and contraction criterion of [NekrashevychSidki2004](References.md#NekrashevychSidki2004) and [Nekrashevych2005](References.md#Nekrashevych2005); we have not found the realization of $P$ itself stated in the literature. **Statement and implications** [Kaplansky1970](References.md#Kaplansky1970), [Passman1977](References.md#Passman1977). |

### <a id="Zassenhaus"></a>Zassenhaus conjecture

| Field | |
|---|---|
| **Problem** | (ZC1) For $G$ **finite**, every unit of finite order in $\mathbb{Z}[G]$ with augmentation 1 is conjugate in $\mathbb{Q}[G]$ to an element of $G$ — equivalently, dropping the normalisation, every torsion unit is rationally conjugate to some $\pm g$. |
| **Status** | `Solved by other means` — **false**, Eisele–Margolis 2018, by a metabelian group of order $2^7 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 5 \cdot 7^2 \cdot 19^2$. |
| **Automaton translation** | `None`, and the reason is structural rather than a gap in the literature. Zassenhaus is about $\mathbb{Z}[G]$ for *finite* $G$, whereas every construction in this catalog — the torsion-free filter, the level tower, supports in a ball of an infinite group — is aimed at infinite groups acting on a rooted tree. Realizing a finite $G$ as an automaton group is possible but empty: embed $G \hookrightarrow S_k$ and let it act on the first level with all sections trivial. The automaton is then a re-encoding of the multiplication table and exposes nothing about $\mathbb{Z}[G]$ that $G$ did not already expose. |
| **Faithfulness** | `None`. |
| **Witness shape** | A finite group $G$ together with a normalized torsion unit of $\mathbb{Z}[G]$ that is not rationally conjugate to any element of $G$. Eisele–Margolis's group has order $2^7 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 5 \cdot 7^2 \cdot 19^2$ and was built representation-theoretically, not found by search — both facts put it out of reach of any Mealy-automaton enumeration. |
| **Test recipe** | **None**, matching `Faithfulness: None`. Every group-ring call in the paclet takes `Modulus -> p` and works in a finite level quotient of an infinite group: there is no integral group ring, no torsion-unit enumeration, and no rational-conjugacy test. Nor would adding them help, for the reason the translation field gives — the automaton encoding of a finite $G$ is its multiplication table with all sections trivial, so a search over automata would be a search over finite groups wearing a costume. ## Torsion and growth

### <a id="BurnsideTorsion"></a>Burnside — infinite finitely generated torsion groups

| Field | |
|---|---|
| **Problem** | Is there an infinite finitely generated group in which every element has finite order? Burnside asked in 1902 whether a finitely generated periodic group must be finite; this is the *general* form, which puts no bound on the orders. It is separated here from the bounded form because automata settle this one and provably cannot settle the other. |
| **Status** | `Solved by an automaton group` — Grigorchuk 1980 (five states, two letters, every element of order a power of $2$) and Gupta–Sidki 1983 (over $p$ letters, a $p$-group). Not the first solution: Golod 1964 built an infinite finitely generated torsion group out of Golod–Šafarevič sixteen years earlier, with no automaton anywhere in it. What the automata added is the size of the certificate — a five-row transition table against a graded-algebra argument — which is why this is the entry the [README's problem table](README.md#-open-problems) leads with. An automaton construction of infinite periodic groups goes back further still, to Aleshin 1972; the bibliography carries only his 1983 free-group paper, so that is a gap for T8. |
| **Automaton translation** | A finite invertible Mealy automaton on $s$ states over $k$ letters whose group $G$ satisfies both halves of the problem, each of which is a statement about the level tower. $G$ is **infinite** iff $\lvert G_L \rvert \to \infty$, since the action is faithful and every $G_L$ is finite. A word $w$ in the states has **finite order** iff its order in $\mathrm{Aut}(T_L)$ — nondecreasing in $L$ — is eventually constant, and $G$ is torsion iff that holds for every $w$. Grigorchuk's witness is $a = \sigma$, $b = (a, c)$, $c = (a, d)$, $d = (1, b)$ — five states over two letters counting the identity state, carried here as the code $\{8950703898, \{5, 2\}\}$; Gupta–Sidki's is $a = (1, 1, 1)\sigma$, $t = (a, a^{-1}, t)$, four states over three letters. The mechanism that makes the torsion half *provable* rather than merely observed is contraction: the level-$1$ stabiliser embeds in $G \times G$ by sections, the sections of a word are shorter than the word, and induction on length bounds the order. That is the only route in this catalog from a finite transition table to a statement quantified over every element of an infinite group. |
| **Faithfulness** | `Equivalent` — an automaton group is a finitely generated group, and the two conditions above are literally the two halves of the problem, turned into statements about the finite quotients by residual finiteness. The limitation is computational rather than mathematical, and it cuts one way only. A finite window of levels can **refute** torsion and never confirm it: a strictly growing order at some level proves infinite order, so "not torsion" has finite certificates, while "torsion" has none — an order can plateau and later resume, as the lamplighter's does, and there are infinitely many words to check. Confirming a witness is therefore a proof obligation, the contraction induction above, and not a search. |
| **Witness shape** | A whole automaton, not an element — the first entry in this catalog where the witness is the presentation itself rather than a coefficient vector over a fixed group. Both known witnesses are small: $s = 5$, $k = 2$ (four involutions and the identity state, with $ab$, $ac$, $ad$ of orders $16$, $8$, $4$), and $s = 4$, $k = 3$. A *refutation* is far cheaper and is a genuine finite certificate: one word $w$ and one level $L$ with $\mathrm{ord}_L(w) < \mathrm{ord}_{L+1}(w)$. Whether a witness exists at $s = 3$, $k = 2$ is answerable from the BGKMNSS classification of the $194$ groups generated by three-state binary automata; this repository has not read it off, and nothing here claims either way. |
| **Test recipe** | Two calls, in the order the argument needs them. `AutomatonWordOrders[automaton, word, levels]` gives $\mathrm{ord}_L(w)$ for each $L$ — nondecreasing in $L$, and constant from some level on exactly when $w$ has finite order — and running it across `AutomatonWordBall[automaton, radius]` is the torsion evidence, one word at a time. `AutomatonTorsionFreeCandidateQ[automaton, wordLength, levels]` reads the same data as a single predicate, and `False` is what this entry wants: it means some word's order plateaued between two well-separated levels. That is why none of the four sweeps ever collected a torsion group — they keep the automata this entry discards, and discard the ones it keeps. Verified on the Grigorchuk automaton, built as `IteratedFiniteAutomatonFromWreath[<|"e" -> {{0, 1}, {"e", "e"}}, "a" -> {{1, 0}, {"e", "e"}}, "b" -> {{0, 1}, {"a", "c"}}, "c" -> {{0, 1}, {"a", "d"}}, "d" -> {{0, 1}, {"e", "b"}}|>, "a"]`: `AutomatonWordOrders` on its `"Rule"` gives $2, 2, 2$ at levels $4, 8, 12$ for each of $a$ and $b$, $8, 16, 16$ for $ab$ and $4, 8, 8$ for $abad$ — every order plateaued, and `AutomatonTorsionFreeCandidateQ[rule, 4, {6, 8}]` is `False`. The contrast is `AutomatonWordOrders[{5125, {3, 2}}, {1, 2}, {2, 4, 6, 8}]` $= 4, 16, 64, 256$, an order quadrupling every second level. The asymmetry is the point and is not fixable here: a plateau is evidence of finite order and never a proof, since orders can resume — the lamplighter is the standing counterexample — while a finite order actually *has* a certificate, $w^n = 1$, which no export can check because that is a word-problem test (see [triviality](#Triviality)). |
| **Searched here** | Grigorchuk's automaton is **verified as a witness, never searched for**. Exhaustively over all $4^n$ words of length $n \leq 6$ in the four generators, evaluated at level $12$, the orders realised are exactly $1, 2, 4, 8, 16$ — a $2$-group across that window, with order $32$ attained by no word of length $\leq 6$, so no order these words reach is an exponent for the group. Sampling agrees: $200$ random words of length $6$ at level $9$ land only on powers of two. The level-depth trap is recorded rather than avoided — order $32$ appears at word length $32$ only from level $10$, and at length $16$ only by level $14$, so a shallow level hides orders that are really there. Both directions are controlled against the adding machine (code $62$ in $(2, 2)$), whose generator has infinite order and whose orbit on the zero tape of length $8$ runs the full $2^8$ steps where every Grigorchuk generator closes in at most two. **No systematic search for infinite torsion groups has ever run here.** All four sweeps applied `AutomatonTorsionFreeCandidateQ`, which *keeps* the automata whose word orders are still growing; the $5699$ automata it discarded in $(3, 2)$, and their counterparts in $(2, 2)$, $(2, 3)$ and $(4, 2)$, were dropped unexamined. A rejection is also much weaker than torsion — it says *one* word's order plateaued at two well-separated levels, where a Burnside witness needs *every* word to have finite order — and the filter's own calibration measures that gap: over $(2, 3)$ the adjacent levels $\{3, 4\}, \{4, 5\}$ leave $41$ of the $231$ classes while the well-separated $\{4, 6\}, \{5, 7\}$ leave $55$, so $14$ classes plateau early and resume later. |
| **References** | **Problem** [Burnside1902](References.md#Burnside1902). **First automaton solution** [Aleshin1972](References.md#Aleshin1972), eight years before the group everyone quotes. **The quoted witnesses** [Grigorchuk1980](References.md#Grigorchuk1980) and [GuptaSidki1983](References.md#GuptaSidki1983); [Golod1964](References.md#Golod1964) for the earlier non-automaton construction, [NovikovAdyan1968a](References.md#NovikovAdyan1968a)–[NovikovAdyan1968c](References.md#NovikovAdyan1968c) and [Adyan1979](References.md#Adyan1979) for the bounded-exponent line this entry does *not* live on. **Growth of the witness** [Grigorchuk1985](References.md#Grigorchuk1985), [ErschlerZheng2018](References.md#ErschlerZheng2018). **Where the automaton class is too small** [KPS2014](References.md#KPS2014) and [GodinKlimann2016](References.md#GodinKlimann2016): a connected *reversible* automaton on 3 states — or on any prime number of states — cannot generate an infinite Burnside group, so the witnesses are necessarily non-reversible. **Machinery** [GNS2000](References.md#GNS2000), [Nekrashevych2005](References.md#Nekrashevych2005), [Zuk2026](References.md#Zuk2026) for the worked Grigorchuk example the recipe rebuilds. |

### <a id="BurnsideExponent"></a>Burnside — smallest bounded exponent

| Field | |
|---|---|
| **Problem** | For which $n$ is there an infinite finitely generated group of exponent $n$ — equivalently, for which $m, n \geq 2$ is the free Burnside group $B(m, n) = F_m / \langle\langle w^n : w \in F_m \rangle\rangle$ infinite? The smallest such $n$ is the open quantity. |
| **Status** | `Open`, and bracketed from both sides. $B(m, n)$ is finite for $n = 2, 3, 4, 6$ — exponent $2$ is elementary abelian, $n = 3$ is Burnside 1902, $n = 4$ Sanov 1940, $n = 6$ M. Hall 1958 — and infinite for odd $n \geq 665$, Adyan 1979, after Novikov–Adyan 1968's $n \geq 4381$; the even exponents were covered later, by Ivanov 1994 and Lysënok 1996. So the answer lies in $\{5, 7, 8, \ldots, 665\}$, and $n = 5$ is the smallest open case. The *restricted* Burnside problem — that for fixed $m, n$ there are only finitely many finite $m$-generator groups of exponent $n$ — is a theorem, Zelmanov 1991 and 1994, and it is what makes this entry's translation empty rather than merely unknown. Sanov, Hall, Ivanov, Lysënok and Zelmanov are all absent from the bibliography; a gap for T8. |
| **Automaton translation** | `None`, and provably so rather than for want of a construction. Automaton groups are residually finite by definition: the action on $T_k$ is faithful and every level quotient is finite, so $\bigcap_L \ker(G \to G_L) = 1$. Zelmanov's solution of the restricted Burnside problem is equivalent to the statement that **every finitely generated residually finite group of finite exponent is finite**. Composing the two: no infinite automaton group has bounded exponent, at any $s$ and any $k$. The bounded Burnside problem is thus outside the reach of the entire formalism this catalog is built on, and the unbounded exponent of the Grigorchuk and Gupta–Sidki groups is *forced* by their residual finiteness rather than being an accident of how they were assembled. |
| **Faithfulness** | `None`. |
| **Witness shape** | On the group side, an infinite finitely generated group of exponent $n$ for some $5 \leq n < 665$ — in practice a proof that $B(2, n)$ is infinite, which is a combinatorial argument about van Kampen diagrams and not a finite object at all. On the automaton side there is **no witness shape at any size**, by the paragraph above. What an automaton can exhibit is the theorem's prediction on an example: $\exp(G_L)$ is computable for each $L$, and for an infinite automaton group it must grow without bound. Zelmanov's proof is not effective, so it supplies no bound on $\lvert G \rvert$ in terms of $n$ that a computation could be checked against. |
| **Test recipe** | **None**, and uniquely among the four empty recipes there is nothing to implement, because there is nothing to find. Automaton groups are residually finite by construction — the action on $T_k$ is faithful and every $G_L$ is finite — and Zelmanov's restricted Burnside theorem makes a finitely generated residually finite group of finite exponent finite. So no infinite automaton group has bounded exponent, at any $s$ and any $k$, and a sweep looking for one would be searching a provably empty set. The runnable residue is a consistency check rather than a search: `AutomatonWordOrders[automaton, word, levels]` over `AutomatonWordBall[automaton, radius]` will always exhibit some word whose order keeps growing, for every infinite automaton group and every bound one might hope to certify — on the Grigorchuk group, orders $2, 16, 8, \ldots$ across the radius-4 ball, unbounded as $L$ grows even though every single element is torsion. |
| **Searched here** | **Nothing, and by this entry's own argument nothing should be.** The only relevant measurement is the exponent data of the [torsion entry](#BurnsideTorsion): over the Grigorchuk group the greatest order among words of length $\leq 6$ is $16$ at level $12$, order $32$ first appears at word length $32$, and if $m(k)$ denotes the least length of a word of order $2^k$ then $m(1) = 1$, $m(2) = m(3) = m(4) = 2$ and $m(5) \geq 7$. The exponent grows with the ball radius instead of saturating, which is what the theorem requires and is the whole of what a computation here contributes. How $m(k)$ grows is an open question the `Burnside` notebook poses and this catalog does not answer. |
| **References** | **Settled exponents** $n = 3$ [Burnside1902](References.md#Burnside1902), $n = 4$ [Sanov1940](References.md#Sanov1940), $n = 6$ [Hall1958](References.md#Hall1958). **Infinite for large $n$** [NovikovAdyan1968a](References.md#NovikovAdyan1968a)–[NovikovAdyan1968c](References.md#NovikovAdyan1968c), [Adyan1970](References.md#Adyan1970), [Adyan1979](References.md#Adyan1979) (odd $n \geq 665$), [Ivanov1994](References.md#Ivanov1994) and [Lysenok1996](References.md#Lysenok1996) (almost all exponents, including even ones). **Why no automaton group can be a witness** [Zelmanov1991](References.md#Zelmanov1991) and [Zelmanov1992](References.md#Zelmanov1992) for the restricted problem, plus residual finiteness of the tree action — [GNS2000](References.md#GNS2000), [Nekrashevych2005](References.md#Nekrashevych2005). [Abert2005](References.md#Abert2005) reaches the same conclusion on the smaller weakly branch class, by a different route. |

### <a id="Milnor"></a>Milnor growth problem

| Field | |
|---|---|
| **Problem** | Milnor asked in 1968 whether the growth function $\gamma_{G, S}(n) = \lvert \{ g \in G : \lvert g \rvert_S \leq n \} \rvert$ of a finitely generated group is always either polynomially or exponentially bounded, and whether polynomial growth forces virtual nilpotence. The second half is Gromov's theorem, 1981. The first half — does *intermediate* growth occur? — is the part automata answered. |
| **Status** | `Solved by an automaton group` — **no**, Grigorchuk 1984 (the Russian original; the bibliography's `Grigorchuk1985` is its translation). The same five-state binary automaton as the [torsion entry](#BurnsideTorsion) generates a group with $e^{\sqrt{n}} \preceq \gamma(n) \prec e^{n}$, the first group of intermediate growth, and the uncountable family $G_\omega^{(p)}$ shows that uncountably many growth types occur. Pinning down $\gamma$ for the Grigorchuk group itself is a separate and much later story — Bartholdi's upper bound, then Erschler–Zheng, narrowing the exponent $\alpha$ in $\gamma(n) = e^{n^{\alpha + o(1)}}$ — and this catalog does not state where that stands, because neither reference is in the bibliography. A gap for T8. |
| **Automaton translation** | For a fixed automaton every individual *value* of the growth function is computable from the level tower, and from below. `AutomatonElementCount[automaton, n, L]` is $\gamma_{G_L, S}(n)$, the number of distinct elements of $\mathrm{Aut}(T_L)$ represented by words of length $\leq n$; since $G_{L+1} \twoheadrightarrow G_L$ it is nondecreasing in $L$, and since the action is faithful $\sup_L \gamma_{G_L}(n) = \gamma_G(n)$ exactly. Two words in the radius-$n$ ball that differ in $G$ are separated at some finite level, so for each fixed $n$ the sequence stabilises at the true value. The growth **type** — which is what Milnor's problem is about — is not reached this way: it is an equivalence class of asymptotics under $\gamma \preceq \delta \iff \gamma(n) \leq \delta(Cn)$, and no finite set of exact values determines it. Grigorchuk's proof does not come from counting either: both the lower bound $e^{\sqrt{n}}$ and the upper bound $e^{n^\alpha}$ come from the branch and contraction structure of the automaton. |
| **Faithfulness** | `Heuristic` for the growth type, which is the problem — even though each individual ball size is exact. Ball counts to any finite radius are consistent with both intermediate and exponential growth, so a subexponential-looking count is an analogy and not evidence; this repository has a documented instance of the analogy failing on its own data, recorded below. The part that *is* exact, and worth keeping separate rather than letting it colour the field: for a fixed automaton and a fixed $n$, the level counts converge upward to $\gamma_G(n)$ and stabilise at it. |
| **Witness shape** | Grigorchuk's five-state binary automaton is the witness, and the uncountable family $G_\omega^{(p)}$ is why growth types are uncountable — but only an eventually periodic $\omega$ gives a *finite* automaton, so the automaton-group scope is the countable subfamily. For a search, a candidate has the shape of an automaton with $\lvert G_L \rvert \to \infty$ whose ball counts $\gamma_{G_L}(n)$ look subexponential in $n$, and the whole point of the field above is that this is a filter and not a certificate. What *is* a finite certificate is the opposite verdict: two elements generating a free sub-semigroup — all $2^n$ of their positive products distinct, witnessed at a single level $L$ — proves exponential growth and removes the candidate. That is exactly how the BGKMNSS classification contradicts this repository's own ball-count label. |
| **Test recipe** | `AutomatonGroupFingerprint[automaton, level]` returns `<|"Code", "Abelian", "BallGrowth"|>`, with `"BallGrowth"` the number of elements of $G_L$ represented by words of length $1$ to $4$ — the four numbers the $(3, 2)$ stratification was cut on. One radius at a time is `AutomatonElementCount[automaton, wordLength, level]`, and the elements themselves are `AutomatonGroupBall[automaton, radius, refLevel]`. **The level argument is the whole subtlety, and this entry is where it is cheapest to demonstrate.** Counts are nondecreasing in $L$ with supremum $\gamma_G(n)$, so a count taken too shallow is a strict lower bound and nothing else. Verified: `AutomatonGroupFingerprint[{5125, {3, 2}}, 8]` gives `"BallGrowth"` $\{7, 37, 187, 937\}$, exactly the free-group values $1 + \sum_{j \leq n} 6 \cdot 5^{\,j-1}$, whereas the same call at the sweeps' reference level $6$ gives $\{7, 37, 181, 855\}$ — $3\%$ and $9\%$ low, inside the sweep's own data. What no call here does is decide a growth *type*: the recipe measures a finite prefix of a sequence and the classification against subexponential or polynomial is done by hand against the literature, which is exactly why `Faithfulness` reads `Heuristic` and why BGKMNSS's exponential-growth proof overrides the "intermediate" label on 11 of the 32 codes. |
| **Searched here** | Two computations: one a witness verification, one a heuristic already known to be wrong. **(a)** The Grigorchuk ball sizes in the level-$9$ quotient are $5, 11, 23, 40, 68, 108$ for $n = 1, \ldots, 6$, against $9, 65, 457, 3201, 22409, 156865$ for the free group on the same four generators — subexponential across that window, and the `Burnside` notebook records it as a `Conjecture` verified for $n \leq 6$ in one quotient, not as a growth theorem. **(b)** Across $(3, 2)$, `AutomatonGroupFingerprint` computed the radius-$1$ through radius-$4$ ball sizes of all $133$ torsion-free candidates, and the sweep stratified them into $36$ abelian, $65$ free-like and $32$ *intermediate*, where "intermediate" means nothing more than *not abelian, and radius-$4$ ball under $700$*. **That label is not a growth claim, and it is false as one:** BGKMNSS proves a free monoid on two generators, hence exponential growth, for classes $849$, $929$, $2841$ and $2850$ — $11$ of the $32$ codes. A third of the stratum is misclassified by its own name, which is the sharpest available argument for the `Heuristic` verdict above. Nothing was computed in $(2, 2)$, $(2, 3)$ or $(4, 2)$ beyond what the torsion filter needed, and no group in any swept space has been shown here to have intermediate growth. |
| **References** | **Problem** [Milnor1968](References.md#Milnor1968). **Solution** [Grigorchuk1985](References.md#Grigorchuk1985), on the automaton of [Grigorchuk1980](References.md#Grigorchuk1980). **The dichotomy at the polynomial end** [Gromov1981](References.md#Gromov1981). **Growth functions actually computed** [BartholdiErschler2010](References.md#BartholdiErschler2010), [ErschlerZheng2018](References.md#ErschlerZheng2018) — the second gives the exponent $\alpha_0 \approx 0.7674$ that a measured ball count would have to be compared against. **Why the "intermediate" label is false on 11 of 32 codes** [BGKMNSS2007a](References.md#BGKMNSS2007a), [BGKMNSS2009](References.md#BGKMNSS2009), [BGKMNSS2008](References.md#BGKMNSS2008); [Aleshin1983](References.md#Aleshin1983) and [VorobetsVorobets2006](References.md#VorobetsVorobets2006) for the free classes at the other extreme. **Surveys** [Zuk2008](References.md#Zuk2008), [Zuk2012](References.md#Zuk2012). |

### <a id="GapConjecture"></a>Gap conjecture

| Field | |
|---|---|
| **Problem** | Grigorchuk's gap conjecture: if a finitely generated group has growth $\gamma(n) \prec e^{\sqrt{n}}$ then it is virtually nilpotent, hence of polynomial growth. Equivalently, $e^{\sqrt{n}}$ is a genuine gap — no growth type lies strictly between polynomial and $e^{\sqrt{n}}$. |
| **Status** | `Open`, Grigorchuk 2014. The bound is not arbitrary: every $G_\omega^{(p)}$ of the intermediate-growth family satisfies $\gamma \succeq e^{\sqrt{n}}$, so the conjecture asserts that those examples are optimal. The paper's contribution is a reduction rather than a proof — validity for residually finite groups implies validity in general, as does validity for simple groups, and likewise for residually polycyclic groups together with just-infinite ones; residually solvable and right-orderable groups are treated there as well. |
| **Automaton translation** | An automaton whose group $G$ is infinite, not virtually nilpotent, and has $\gamma_G(n) \prec e^{\sqrt{n}}$. This is the one entry where the automaton class is not a convenient testbed but the right one: automaton groups are residually finite by construction, and residually finite is one of the two classes the conjecture reduces to, so the family this paclet enumerates sits *inside* the reduction rather than beside it. Growth values come off the level tower exactly as in the [Milnor entry](#Milnor). |
| **Faithfulness** | `Sufficient` — a counterexample among automaton groups refutes the conjecture outright, being a counterexample among finitely generated groups. Absence proves nothing, for the usual reason that a sweep bounds only the automata it enumerated and for a sharper one: automaton groups are a *proper* subclass of the residually finite groups, so the reduction runs the other way and no amount of automaton evidence confirms the conjecture. Recognising a witness is not a finite computation either — $\gamma \prec e^{\sqrt{n}}$ is asymptotic and the level counts are lower bounds — so the search side of this entry is a candidate filter and not a decision procedure. |
| **Witness shape** | An automaton group that is infinite, not virtually nilpotent, and of growth below $e^{\sqrt{n}}$. Every known intermediate-growth automaton group sits at or above the bound, so a witness cannot be a variant of the Grigorchuk family — that is the content of the conjecture. The checkable surrogates are both negative: a free sub-semigroup on two elements removes a candidate by forcing exponential growth, and a finite nucleus (`AutomatonNucleus` stabilising in the *radius* direction) is the structural property every known intermediate-growth example has, so it is where a search would filter first. The scale of the positive side is worth stating plainly: $e^{\sqrt{n}}$ does not overtake even $n^3$ until $n = 289$, where the ball already holds $\approx 2.4 \times 10^7$ elements. Any ball count able to separate the two hypotheses lives at word lengths in the hundreds. |
| **Test recipe** | The same three growth calls as [Milnor](#Milnor) — `AutomatonGroupFingerprint`, `AutomatonElementCount`, `AutomatonGroupBall` — and nothing beyond them, since no export addresses an asymptotic bound. The scale makes this a recipe on paper: separating $e^{\sqrt{n}}$ from a polynomial needs $n$ in the hundreds ($e^{\sqrt{n}}$ does not pass $n^3$ until $n = 289$, where the ball would hold $\approx 2.4 \times 10^7$ elements), against the radius $4$ that `"BallGrowth"` returns and the radius $3$ that is comfortable at level $8$. What *is* runnable is candidate selection, and it is what leaves one candidate standing among the 32. `AutomatonNucleus[automaton, radius, depth, refLevel]` picks out the contracting codes; `AutomatonAbelianQ[automaton, level]` together with `"BallGrowth"` separates the virtually nilpotent ones, which the gap conjecture already covers; and the Basilica group is what remains — `Map[Length, AutomatonNucleus[{7964, {3, 2}}, 3, 4, 6]]` returns $\{53, 25, 15, 11, 7\}$, the contracting signature (see [amenability of a given automaton](#AmenabilityGiven) for how to read that list, which is less obvious than it looks). A filter, not a test: it says which automaton to think about, and nothing about its growth. |
| **Searched here** | **Nothing directed at this conjecture.** The relevant data is the $(3, 2)$ ball-growth stratification of the [Milnor entry](#Milnor), which reaches radius $4$ — a ball of at most a few hundred elements, against the $n = 289$ and $\approx 2.4 \times 10^7$ elements a separation would need — together with the nucleus computation over the $32$ intermediate codes, which found only $2$ of the $14$ isomorphism classes contracting: the Basilica group and the Klein bottle group. That is the closest thing here to a candidate filter, and it leaves two identified groups rather than an unknown. The Klein bottle group is virtually $\mathbb{Z}^2$, so its growth is polynomial and it is not a candidate. The Basilica group is not virtually nilpotent, so it is a candidate exactly if its growth lies below $e^{\sqrt{n}}$ — nothing computed in this repository bears on that, and the question is raised nowhere else in it. |

## Amenability and $L^2$-invariants

### <a id="VonNeumannDay"></a>von Neumann–Day problem

| Field | |
|---|---|
| **Problem** | Must every non-amenable group contain a free subgroup of rank $2$? A group is amenable if it carries a left-invariant mean, the notion von Neumann introduced in 1929, and a rank-$2$ free subgroup is the obvious obstruction; Day 1957 studied the class systematically and the question is attached to both names. |
| **Status** | `Solved by other means` — **no**. Ol'shanskii 1980, with Tarski monsters: infinite groups in which every proper subgroup is cyclic of order $p$, non-amenable and free-subgroup-free for the trivial reason. Adyan 1983, with the free Burnside groups $B(m, n)$ of the [bounded Burnside entry](#BurnsideExponent), which are non-amenable. Both are infinitely presented and hand-built. Monod 2013 later gave far simpler counterexamples — groups of piecewise projective homeomorphisms of the line — and Lodha–Moore 2013 the first finitely presented torsion-free one; neither is in the bibliography, a gap for T8. |
| **Automaton translation** | An automaton whose group is non-amenable and contains no rank-$2$ free subgroup. **Neither counterexample cited above can be an automaton group**, and the argument is the [bounded Burnside entry](#BurnsideExponent)'s: a Tarski monster has exponent $p$ and $B(m, n)$ has exponent $n$, so both have finite exponent, while an automaton group is residually finite and Zelmanov's restricted Burnside theorem leaves no infinite finitely generated residually finite group of finite exponent. So the missing automaton counterexample is not a matter of nobody having tried to realize the known ones. Inside the automaton class the two halves of a witness pull against each other, which is the real content here. *No free subgroup* is the cheap half: Nekrashevych 2008 proves iterated monodromy groups of expanding coverings have none, and reproves a theorem of Sidki giving the same for groups generated by bounded automata — but bounded activity already implies **amenability** ([activity entry](#AmenabilityActivity)), so that route is closed by its own hypothesis. What is left is the expanding-IMG side, where free subgroups are excluded and amenability is not known in general: **a non-amenable iterated monodromy group of an expanding covering would be a von Neumann counterexample.** |
| **Faithfulness** | `Sufficient`. The problem is settled, so an automaton witness would not decide it; what it would supply is a self-similar counterexample, which no construction gives. Absence proves nothing, and it is worth being exact about which absence, because two different ones are available to be overread: a sweep that turns up no candidate bounds only the automata enumerated, and — more basic — **no computation in this catalog decides amenability of even one automaton group** ([given-automaton entry](#AmenabilityGiven)), so "no candidate found" here never means "candidates were tested and rejected". |
| **Witness shape** | An automaton group that is non-amenable with no rank-$2$ free subgroup — by the paragraph above, most plausibly a contracting or expanding-IMG automaton that is *not* of bounded activity, since bounded activity would make it amenable. Contraction is the one half of that shape this repository can compute, as `AutomatonNucleus` stabilising in the radius direction. Non-amenability has no finite certificate at all: the one certificate anybody uses is a free subgroup, and a free subgroup is exactly what the witness must not have. The group-side counterexamples set no size scale for the automaton side — Ol'shanskii's and Adyan's groups are infinitely presented, Monod's are homeomorphism groups, and none of them is finite-state. |
| **Test recipe** | **A filter only, and it runs on the obstruction rather than on the problem.** No export tests amenability, and none exhibits a free subgroup — that would need a word-problem certificate on the ball, which no export computes (see [triviality](#Triviality)). What is computable is which automata the two halves of the argument apply to. `AutomatonNucleus[automaton, radius, depth, refLevel]` decides contraction, and contraction is *necessary* for bounded activity (Bondarenko–Nekrashevych), so a non-contracting automaton is provably not bounded and therefore escapes both Sidki's no-free-subgroups theorem and `BKN2010`'s amenability. That is the test that put 28 of the 32 torsion-free $(3, 2)$ codes outside both halves. Verified, and it is also the caution: `Map[Length, AutomatonNucleus[{5125, {3, 2}}, 3, 4, 6]]` returns $\{181, 181, 181, 181, 181\}$ — constant, and $181$ is the size of the radius-3 ball itself, so the sections of the ball are the ball again and nothing contracts. The Aleshin automaton generates a free group and is the most emphatically non-contracting automaton in the space, yet its list is *flat*; contraction shows up as the counts **decreasing** to a limit that does not grow with the radius, not as the list holding still. `AutomatonWordOrders` supplies the other filter: both cited counterexamples have finite exponent, so an unbounded order along the tower already disqualifies an automaton group from being either of them. |
| **Searched here** | **Nothing; no amenability or non-amenability computation has ever run in this repository.** The one datum that bears on the witness shape is the contraction flag of the [gap-conjecture entry](#GapConjecture): of the $32$ torsion-free $(3, 2)$ codes, $4$ are contracting — the Basilica pair $7964, 7974$ and the Klein bottle pair $4117, 4621$ — and $28$ are not, so by Bondarenko–Nekrashevych 2007 those $28$ are not bounded automata either. Of the four, three are bounded by inspection of the recursion, hence amenable and not candidates: $7964$ has $b = \sigma(c, 1)$, $c = (b, 1)$; $7974$ has $b = \sigma(c, 1)$, $c = (1, b)$; $4621$ has $a = \sigma(1, a)$, $c = \sigma(a, 1)$. The fourth, $4117$ with $a = \sigma(b, a)$, $b = (a, a)$, $c = \sigma(a, b)$, is contracting with activity $\alpha_n = 2^n$ — the one shape in the swept space this entry would look at twice — and its group is the Klein bottle group, solvable and so amenable. **The swept space therefore contains no candidate at all**, which is a fact about a space of $32$ automata and not about the problem. |
| **References** | **Problem** attributed to [Neumann1929](References.md#Neumann1929) and posed in the form used here by [Day1957](References.md#Day1957). **Counterexamples** [Olshanskii1980](References.md#Olshanskii1980) (Tarski monsters, exponent $p$) and [Adyan1983](References.md#Adyan1983) (free Burnside groups, exponent $n$) — both of finite exponent, hence not automaton groups by [Zelmanov1991](References.md#Zelmanov1991)/[Zelmanov1992](References.md#Zelmanov1992); [Monod2013](References.md#Monod2013) and [LodhaMoore2013](References.md#LodhaMoore2013) are the torsion-free counterexamples that escape that argument. **Why the two halves cancel inside the bounded class** [Sidki2000](References.md#Sidki2000) and [Nekrashevych2008](References.md#Nekrashevych2008) (no free subgroups), against [BKN2010](References.md#BKN2010) (amenability). **The filter** [BN2007](References.md#BN2007) — bounded implies contracting, so `AutomatonNucleus` is a negative test. **The claim that is false** [Abert2005](References.md#Abert2005): weakly branch groups satisfy no law, so they are not free-subgroup-free. |

### <a id="DayElementary"></a>Day — is every amenable group elementarily amenable?

| Field | |
|---|---|
| **Problem** | Is every amenable group elementarily amenable? The class $\mathrm{EG}$ of elementary amenable groups is the smallest one containing the finite and the abelian groups and closed under subgroups, quotients, extensions and directed unions — Day 1957 isolated it, and everything in it is amenable by construction. The question is whether the construction reaches everything. |
| **Status** | `Solved by an automaton group` — **no**, Grigorchuk 1984, with the five-state binary automaton of the [torsion](#BurnsideTorsion) and [Milnor](#Milnor) entries. Both halves come from its growth: subexponential growth gives a Følner sequence, hence amenability, and Chou 1980 proved that a finitely generated elementary amenable group has either polynomial or exponential growth, so *intermediate* growth is by itself a proof of non-membership in $\mathrm{EG}$. Chou is absent from the bibliography, a gap for T8. Basilica sharpens the answer and shrinks the automaton to three states over two letters: it is amenable (Bartholdi–Virág 2005) and not even *subexponentially* amenable (Grigorchuk–Żuk 2002), so it lies outside the larger class $\mathrm{SG} \supseteq \mathrm{EG}$ got by closing the subexponentially growing groups under the same four operations. |
| **Automaton translation** | An automaton whose group is amenable and not elementarily amenable. The Grigorchuk route is the [Milnor entry](#Milnor)'s translation with its verdict inherited: intermediate growth delivers both halves at once by the two theorems above, and the automaton side of intermediate growth is a heuristic, so this route is no more computable than that one. The Basilica route is structural instead, and it is *not* reachable by any ball count — Basilica cannot have subexponential growth, since that would place it in $\mathrm{SG}$, so its growth is exponential and no count of a ball can distinguish it from a free group. That is the useful shape of the entry: the sharper witness is the one whose amenability is invisible to counting. |
| **Faithfulness** | `Sufficient` — an automaton witness settles the problem, and one did. Absence proves nothing, and the asymmetry runs deeper than in the growth entries because *recognising* a witness is not a finite problem on either half. Amenability of a given automaton group has no decision procedure ([given-automaton entry](#AmenabilityGiven)), and non-membership in $\mathrm{EG}$ is established by exhibiting some property no group in $\mathrm{EG}$ has, where the only property used so far — intermediate growth — is asymptotic. |
| **Witness shape** | Grigorchuk's five states over two letters, or Basilica's three, carried here as $(3, 2)$ codes $7964$ ($b = \sigma(c, 1)$, $c = (b, 1)$) and $7974$ (its mirror, $c = (1, b)$). Both are **bounded** automata, which is the one structural feature the two amenability proofs share even though the arguments do not — Basilica's is a random walk, and it was generalized to the whole bounded class five years later ([activity entry](#AmenabilityActivity)). So a candidate has the shape of a bounded automaton whose group is not elementarily amenable: bounded settles the amenability half by theorem, and all the work moves to the $\mathrm{EG}$ side, where this repository has no surrogate at all. |
| **Test recipe** | **No test, and none is possible with these exports** — elementary amenability is a closure property of a class of groups, not a property of a ball, and nothing in the paclet ranges over subgroups, quotients or directed unions. The recipe is therefore a consistency check, and it is important to say what it is not evidence for. `AutomatonGroupFingerprint[automaton, level]` and `AutomatonElementCount[automaton, wordLength, level]` measure ball growth, and subexponential growth *implies* amenability while carrying no information about elementary amenability; on the Grigorchuk automaton `AutomatonGroupFingerprint[rule, 8]` returns `"BallGrowth"` $\{5, 11, 23, 40\}$ — the five-state presentation including the trivial state — which is consistent with intermediate growth and consistent with a great many other things, at radius 4. The one genuinely computable ingredient of the actual argument is torsion: `AutomatonWordOrders` over `AutomatonWordBall` exhibits the finite orders that make the Grigorchuk group a torsion group, and torsion plus non-elementary-amenability is what the citation supplies. The Basilica sharpening is out of reach for the same reason — "not subexponentially amenable" is a statement about a class, not about a computation. |
| **Searched here** | **Nothing.** No amenability computation exists here and no elementary-amenability test either. What is on record is the growth data of the [Milnor entry](#Milnor) — Grigorchuk ball sizes $5, 11, 23, 40, 68, 108$ for $n \leq 6$ in the level-$9$ quotient — which is consistent with the intermediate growth driving this entry and, by that entry's own `Heuristic` verdict, is not evidence for it. Basilica appears in the swept $(3, 2)$ space as codes $7964, 7974$, identified through the BGKMNSS classification and not by anything computed here about amenability or growth. |
| **References** | **Problem** [Day1957](References.md#Day1957); the class and its closure properties [Chou1980](References.md#Chou1980). **Solution** [Grigorchuk1985](References.md#Grigorchuk1985) on the automaton of [Grigorchuk1980](References.md#Grigorchuk1980) — subexponential growth gives amenability, torsion and non-elementary-amenability give the separation. **Sharpened** [GrigorchukZuk2002](References.md#GrigorchukZuk2002) and [BartholdiVirag2005](References.md#BartholdiVirag2005): Basilica is amenable but not even subexponentially amenable. **Unified proofs covering both** [JNS2016](References.md#JNS2016), with [BKN2010](References.md#BKN2010) and [AAV2013](References.md#AAV2013) for the activity-graded versions. |

### <a id="AmenabilityActivity"></a>Amenability by activity growth

| Field | |
|---|---|
| **Problem** | Sidki 2000 grades the finite-state automorphisms of $T_k$ by *activity*: $\alpha_n(g) = \#\{ v \text{ at level } n : g\vert_v \neq 1 \}$, with $g$ **bounded** when $\alpha_n(g)$ is bounded and of **polynomial activity of degree $d$** when $\alpha_n(g) = O(n^d)$; each degree is a group. Is every group generated by an automaton of polynomial activity amenable? |
| **Status** | `Partially solved`, degree by degree. Degree $0$ — bounded — **yes**, Bartholdi–Kaimanovich–Nekrashevych 2010, by reducing to an explicit finite family of "mother groups" and analysing random walks on them. Degree $1$ — linear — **yes**, Amir–Angel–Virág 2013 (arXiv 2009), which answers a question of Nekrashevych in the Kourovka notebook and part of one of Sidki's. Degree $\geq 2$ is **open**. Juschenko–Nekrashevych–de la Salle 2016 (arXiv 2013) recovers both from one criterion, recurrence of the orbital Schreier graphs. The hierarchy is not vacuous at the top: the Aleshin automaton has exponential activity and generates $F_3$, which is non-amenable. Amir–Angel–Virág and Juschenko–Nekrashevych–de la Salle are both missing from the bibliography — the gap T1 flagged, now needing two references rather than one. |
| **Automaton translation** | This entry *is* a translation, run in the direction that pays: a condition read off the transition table implies a property of the group. Boundedness is decidable and purely combinatorial — by Sidki's criterion an automaton is bounded exactly when no two distinct cycles through nontrivial states of its Moore diagram are joined by a directed path, the trivial state's loop excluded. Two facts fix the scope. Bondarenko–Nekrashevych 2007 proved every self-similar action by bounded automata is **contracting**, so contraction is *necessary* for boundedness and a non-contracting code is provably not bounded — which is what makes this repository's nucleus data usable here at all. And activity is an invariant of the automaton, not of the abstract group: one group can have a bounded presentation and infinitely many that are not, so a negative verdict is never a statement about the group. |
| **Faithfulness** | `Sufficient` — bounded or linear activity implies amenability, and it is a theorem, so a *positive* reading is sound in a way nothing else in this section is: no search, no bound, no level ceiling. It does not run backwards at any degree. Exponential activity is perfectly consistent with amenability, and no bound on the degree is known to be necessary. At degree $\geq 2$ there is no theorem at all, so restricted to those automata the honest value is `None`. |
| **Witness shape** | Two shapes in opposite directions. To *extend* the theorem: a non-amenable automaton group of polynomial activity of degree $\geq 2$ refutes the general statement, and a proof at degree $2$ is the next link in the chain. To *use* it: a bounded automaton, which is a finite check on the Moore diagram. The known examples are all small and all bounded — the adding machine $a = \sigma(1, a)$ has $\alpha_n \equiv 1$; Basilica's $b = \sigma(c, 1)$, $c = (b, 1)$ has $\alpha_n \equiv 1$; and Grigorchuk's nontrivial states form the single cycle $b \to c \to d \to b$ with $a$ finitary, so it is bounded too. |
| **Test recipe** | **No activity function exists**, which is the third empty recipe and the most frustrating of the four: `AutomatonSectionTable`, `AutomatonSection` and `IteratedFiniteAutomatonStateGraph` hold exactly the data Sidki's degree needs, and nothing assembles them into one. A *lower bound* is three lines over the existing exports and does run — count the depth-$d$ sections that act nontrivially at a reference level, `Count[Map[Function[v, AutomatonWordPermutation[automaton, refLevel, AutomatonSection[automaton, word, v]]], Tuples[Range[0, k - 1], d]], p_ /; p =!= Range[k^refLevel]]`. Verified on the lamplighter, `{54, {2, 2}}` with `word = {1}` and `refLevel = 4`: the counts are $1, 2, 4, 8, 16$ for $d = 0, \ldots, 4$, i.e. $2^d$, so activity is *full* and the automaton is nowhere near bounded — which is the correct answer and reached without a word-problem test, because a lower bound only needs sections it can prove nontrivial. That asymmetry is exactly why this is a bound and not the degree: proving activity *small* means proving sections trivial, and a section that is trivial at every level tested may still be nontrivial in $G$ (see [triviality](#Triviality)). Until then the runnable proxy is contraction — `AutomatonNucleus`, necessary for boundedness by Bondarenko–Nekrashevych — which is how 28 of the 32 codes were ruled out of `BKN2010`'s reach without any activity computation at all. |
| **Searched here** | **Nothing, and nothing can be with the symbols that exist: the paclet has no activity function.** `AutomatonSectionTable` and `IteratedFiniteAutomatonStateGraph` between them hold exactly the data Sidki's criterion needs, and no exported symbol assembles them into an activity degree — so bounded activity, the one general *and decidable* sufficient condition for amenability, is not computable here. That makes this the second entry whose `Test recipe` T6 cannot write against existing exports, after the [unit conjecture](#KaplanskyUnit). What existing data does yield is a negative, through Bondarenko–Nekrashevych: of the $32$ torsion-free $(3, 2)$ codes, `AutomatonNucleus` found $28$ non-contracting, so **none of those $28$ is a bounded automaton and BKN 2010 cannot apply to any of them as presented.** Of the remaining four, $7964$, $7974$ and $4621$ are bounded by inspection and $4117$ is contracting with $\alpha_n = 2^n$. So the theorem covers exactly **$3$ of the $32$ codes swept, and tells this repository nothing new**: two are the Basilica pair, where Bartholdi–Virág 2005 got there first by hand, and the third generates the Klein bottle group, which is amenable because it is solvable. |
| **References** | **The hierarchy** [Sidki2000](References.md#Sidki2000). **Degree 0** [BKN2010](References.md#BKN2010); **degree 1** [AAV2013](References.md#AAV2013); **a unified proof of both** [JNS2016](References.md#JNS2016); **degree $\geq 2$ open**. **Why contraction is the computable proxy** [BN2007](References.md#BN2007) — bounded activity implies contracting, so a non-contracting code is provably unbounded. **Where an activity notion is again the gate** [DRW2024](References.md#DRW2024), whose decidability result for finiteness is stated for bounded *extended* activity. **Machinery** [Nekrashevych2005](References.md#Nekrashevych2005), [GNS2000](References.md#GNS2000). |

### <a id="AmenabilityGiven"></a>Amenability of a given Mealy automaton

| Field | |
|---|---|
| **Problem** | Given a finite invertible Mealy automaton, is the group its states generate amenable? This is the Day problem restricted to groups that arrive with a finite presentation of a very strong kind — the whole group is determined by an $s \times k$ transition table — so the input is finite even though the group is not. |
| **Status** | `Open`. No algorithm is known, and no proof that none exists: amenability of automaton groups is not known to be decidable and not known to be undecidable, which distinguishes it from the [finiteness problem](#Finiteness). For individual automata the answer is often known, and always by an argument tailored to that automaton rather than by a procedure — solvability for the Baumslag–Solitar and Klein bottle codes, bounded activity for Basilica and Grigorchuk, an exhibited free subgroup for the Aleshin automaton. |
| **Automaton translation** | `Equivalent`, and trivially so — the group *is* the automaton's group, and there is nothing to translate. The entry earns its place by making the cost of that visible: a tautological translation transmits the difficulty instead of reducing it, and all the usable content sits in one-directional surrogates. Amenable if the activity is bounded or linear ([activity entry](#AmenabilityActivity)). Non-amenable if two words are shown to generate a rank-$2$ free subgroup — and note that a free *monoid* is not enough, since all $2^n$ positive products being distinct is compatible with amenability, which is exactly the trap the [Milnor entry](#Milnor) documents. No free subgroup if the group is an expanding IMG (Nekrashevych 2008) or generated by a bounded automaton (Sidki). None of the three is complete, and the last two are why the [von Neumann entry](#VonNeumannDay) has no candidate to offer. |
| **Faithfulness** | `Equivalent` — and the value is doing no work at all, which is the point of listing it. Neither side has a decision procedure, so this equivalence reduces nothing. Compare the [activity entry](#AmenabilityActivity), whose merely `Sufficient` translation is strictly more useful than this `Equivalent` one: in this catalog the strength of the field and the usefulness of the entry are not the same quantity. |
| **Witness shape** | For amenability: a bounded or linear-activity Moore diagram, or a solvable-by-finite structure — all of them structural, none of them a search, and none verifiable by any amount of level data. For non-amenability: two words generating a free subgroup, certified by exhibiting a level at which no nonempty reduced product of them up to the searched length acts trivially — a finite certificate only relative to that length, so a partial one, and it certifies freeness of a subgroup rather than non-amenability directly. **There is no witness shape for "amenable" that a finite computation can verify.** |
| **Test recipe** | **No algorithm is known and none is implemented**; what runs is the one decidable sufficient condition's prerequisite. `AutomatonNucleus[automaton, radius, depth, refLevel]` gives, for each $d = 0, \ldots, \text{depth}$, the depth-$d$ sections of the words of length $\leq \text{radius}$, kept distinct as automorphisms of $T_{\text{refLevel}}$; contraction shows as those counts **decreasing** to a limit that does not grow when `radius` grows, and the limit is the nucleus. Read it that way and not as "the list stops changing", which is the trap: `Map[Length, AutomatonNucleus[{5125, {3, 2}}, 3, 4, 6]]` is $\{181, 181, 181, 181, 181\}$ — perfectly stable, and non-contracting, $181$ being the size of the radius-3 ball, whereas the contracting Basilica code gives $\{53, 25, 15, 11, 7\}$ and the adding machine $\{7, 5, 3, 3, 3\}$. Contraction is necessary for bounded activity, so a code that fails it is outside `BKN2010`; a code that passes it still has to be bounded by inspection of the recursion, which `AutomatonWreathRecursion[automaton, Method -> "String"]` prints in the form that inspection is done in — `{54, {2, 2}}` renders as `a = σ(b, a)`, `b = (b, b)` for the pair adding-machine/lamplighter. Beyond that the entry is a citation lookup, not a computation: amenability is recorded for 4 of the 14 $(3, 2)$ classes and every one of those 4 came from a paper. |
| **Searched here** | **Nothing.** Amenability is known for $4$ of the $14$ isomorphism classes the $(3, 2)$ sweep identified, in every case by citation and never by computation: $BS(1, 3)$ (codes $10570$, $11290$, $22410$, $23120$), $BS(1, -3)$ ($4265$, $4985$, $22395$, $22940$) and the Klein bottle group ($4117$, $4621$) are solvable and so amenable, and Basilica ($7964$, $7974$) is amenable by Bartholdi–Virág 2005 — $12$ of the $32$ codes. For the other $10$ classes — $741$, $777$ (Brunner–Sidki–Vieira), $849$, $929$, $2199$, $2203$, $2239$, $2398$ (Dahmani), $2841$, $2850$, so $20$ codes — **this repository records no amenability status in either direction.** That is a statement about the repository and not about the literature: BGKMNSS I–II may well settle some of them, and nobody here has read it off. |
| **References** | **No algorithm known**; the sufficient conditions are [BKN2010](References.md#BKN2010), [AAV2013](References.md#AAV2013) and [JNS2016](References.md#JNS2016), and the prerequisite that is computable here is contraction, [BN2007](References.md#BN2007). **The classes with a recorded answer** [GrigorchukZuk2002](References.md#GrigorchukZuk2002) and [BartholdiVirag2005](References.md#BartholdiVirag2005) (Basilica), [BSV1999](References.md#BSV1999) and [Dahmani2005](References.md#Dahmani2005) among the $(3, 2)$ classes, with [BGKMNSS2007a](References.md#BGKMNSS2007a), [BGKMNSS2009](References.md#BGKMNSS2009) and [BGKMNSS2008](References.md#BGKMNSS2008) as the census the "4 of 14" is counted against. **Surveys** [Zuk2008](References.md#Zuk2008), [Zuk2012](References.md#Zuk2012). |

### <a id="Atiyah"></a>Atiyah conjecture

| Field | |
|---|---|
| **Problem** | Atiyah 1976 introduced the $L^2$-Betti numbers of the universal cover of a closed manifold, via an index theorem for a $\Gamma$-invariant elliptic operator, and asked which values they can take — in particular whether they must be rational, and integral when $\pi_1$ is torsion-free. The algebraic form is the **strong Atiyah conjecture**: for a matrix $A$ over $\mathbb{C}[G]$, $\dim_{\mathcal{L}(G)} \ker A$ lies in the additive subgroup of $\mathbb{Q}$ generated by $\{ 1/\lvert H \rvert : H \leq G \text{ finite} \}$, which for torsion-free $G$ is $\mathbb{Z}$. |
| **Status** | `Solved by an automaton group` — **false**, Grigorchuk–Linnell–Schick–Żuk 2000, using the lamplighter group $\mathbb{Z}/2 \wr \mathbb{Z}$: all its finite subgroups are elementary abelian $2$-groups, so the conjecture predicts kernel dimensions in $\mathbb{Z}[1/2]$, and they exhibit a matrix over $\mathbb{Q}[G]$ with $\dim_{\mathcal{L}(G)} \ker = 1/3$. The lamplighter is the **two-state, two-letter** automaton group of Grigorchuk–Żuk 2001, whose spectral computation is what makes the dimension computable at all — the smallest automaton in this catalog, and the shortest route anywhere in it from a finite table to a named conjecture. The sequels keep the same group: Austin 2009 obtained an *irrational* kernel dimension, and Grabowski 2010 and Pichot–Schick–Żuk 2010 transcendental $L^2$-Betti numbers of closed manifolds, Grabowski's explicit example living over the threefold direct product of the lamplighter. **For torsion-free $G$ the integrality statement is open**, and that is the case every automaton in this catalog's sweeps belongs to. Austin, Grabowski and Pichot–Schick–Żuk are absent from the bibliography, a gap for T8. |
| **Automaton translation** | Two routes, one algebraic and one spectral. *(i) Zero divisors.* For torsion-free $G$ the strong Atiyah conjecture implies $\mathbb{C}[G]$ is a domain, by an argument short enough to carry here: for $0 \neq a \in \mathbb{C}[G]$, right multiplication $R_a$ on $\ell^2 G$ has $\dim \ker R_a \leq 1$ with equality only when $a = 0$, since von Neumann dimension is faithful — so integrality forces $\dim \ker R_a = 0$, i.e. $\ker R_a = 0$, i.e. $a$ is not a zero divisor. A zero divisor in $\mathbb{C}[G]$ for a torsion-free automaton group therefore refutes strong Atiyah *for that group*, which is the precise sense in which the [zero-divisor entry](#KaplanskyZeroDivisor) is this one's algebraic shadow. *(ii) Level tower.* The kernel dimension is itself a limit over the tower the paclet already builds: the level stabilisers $\mathrm{St}(L)$ are finite-index normal subgroups with $\bigcap_L \mathrm{St}(L) = 1$, so Lück's approximation theorem gives, for $A$ over $\mathbb{Z}[G]$, $\dim_{\mathcal{L}(G)} \ker A = \lim_L \frac{1}{\lvert G_L \rvert} \dim_{\mathbb{C}} \ker \rho_L(A)$ with $\rho_L$ the regular representation of the finite quotient $G_L$. Every term is a rank computation in a finite group ring — the object `GroupRingLevelProduct` and `GroupRingLeftAnnihilator` already work in — so every $L^2$-Betti number of an automaton group is an explicit limit of computable rationals. |
| **Faithfulness** | `Sufficient`, on route (i): a zero divisor over $\mathbb{C}$ for a torsion-free automaton group refutes the conjecture for that group, and absence proves nothing. Route (ii) is weaker than its exactness suggests and must not be read as `Equivalent` — Lück's theorem gives convergence but no rate, so no finite window of levels certifies that the limit is non-integral, and taken alone route (ii) rates `Heuristic`. The two routes also differ in what they can reach: route (i) applies only where $G$ is torsion-free, which is where the conjecture is still open, while every known counterexample needed torsion to produce the offending denominator. |
| **Witness shape** | On route (i), exactly the [zero-divisor entry](#KaplanskyZeroDivisor)'s witness — a pair of coefficient vectors over a support ball — but over $\mathbb{C}$ or $\mathbb{Q}$, not over $\mathbb{F}_p$, and that distinction is the entire content of `Searched here`. On route (ii), a matrix over $\mathbb{Z}[G]$ together with the sequence $\frac{1}{\lvert G_L \rvert} \dim \ker \rho_L(A)$; GLSZ's matrix is small and its limit is $1/3$, so the shape to look for is a sequence of rationals settling on a denominator that no finite subgroup of $G$ explains. In the torsion-free case any limit in $(0, 1)$ is a counterexample, so the target is simply a non-integer. |

## Decision problems

### <a id="Finiteness"></a>Finiteness problem

| Field | |
|---|---|
| **Problem** | Given a finite Mealy automaton, is the group generated by its states finite? The group acts faithfully on $T_k$ with finite level quotients $G_L$, so the question is whether the nondecreasing tower $\lvert G_1 \rvert \leq \lvert G_2 \rvert \leq \cdots$ is bounded. |
| **Status** | `Open` for automaton **groups**, and the sharpness of that is this entry's content, because the problems on either side of it are settled and in opposite directions. For automaton *semigroups* it is undecidable — Gillibert 2014 builds, from an NW-deterministic Wang tile set, a Mealy automaton whose semigroup is finite iff the plane admits a valid tiling, and Kari had proved that tiling problem unsolvable. A strictly more general form of the *group* problem is undecidable too: D'Angeli–Francoeur–Rodaro–Wächter 2020 show it for finiteness of left principal ideals in semigroups generated by complete reversible automata. The positive results are all small or structural: Klimann 2013 decides finiteness for groups generated by two-state or two-letter invertible-reversible automata, via the dichotomy that a semigroup generated by a reversible two-state automaton is either finite or free of rank $2$; D'Angeli–Rodaro–Wächter 2024 decides it for complete automaton semigroups of bounded extended activity. |
| **Automaton translation** | Two equivalent forms, one useless and one usable, and telling them apart is the whole of the entry. *(i) Level tower.* $G$ is infinite iff $\lvert G_L \rvert \to \infty$: any $n$ distinct elements are separated at some finite level, so $\sup_L \lvert G_L \rvert = \lvert G \rvert$. *(ii) Ball closure.* $G$ is finite iff its ball closes — some radius $r$ has $B_{r+1} = B_r$ **in $G$** — and each equality needed to check that is decidable, being an instance of the [triviality entry](#Triviality)'s word problem. Form (ii) is a semi-algorithm; form (i) is not, since no level ever settles a supremum. What must not be confused with either is ball closure *in a level quotient*, which is automatic: every $G_L$ is finite, so every ball closes in it at some radius. The adding machine is the two-line refutation of that reading — $G_L \cong \mathbb{Z}/2^L$, so the radius-$2^{L-1}$ ball is all of $G_L$ and closes, while $G \cong \mathbb{Z}$. |
| **Faithfulness** | `Equivalent` for both forms above, with an asymmetry that decides what a computation here can mean. Finiteness is **semi-decidable** by form (ii); infiniteness has no finite certificate known, and *if* the group problem turns out to be undecidable then it can have none, because a problem semi-decidable on both sides is decidable. So the two verdicts are not symmetric and never will be by any refinement of the same machinery: "the ball closed" can be a proof, "the ball kept growing" cannot. |
| **Witness shape** | Finite: a radius $r$ and the closed ball, each of its $\lvert G \rvert$ elements a finite-state automorphism and each equality discharged by a section-closure check. The certificate is therefore as large as the group and nothing computable from the automaton bounds it. Infinite: no witness shape, by the field above. The two routes actually used in the literature both go outside this frame — exhibit an element of infinite order, which is the [order problem](#Order) and undecidable, or exhibit a free sub-semigroup, which is a proof and not a check. |
| **Test recipe** | **A filter, never a certificate**, and the recipe is worth writing mainly to show why. `AutomatonElementCount[automaton, wordLength, level]` counts the elements of $G_L$ represented by words of length $\leq \text{wordLength}$; if it keeps rising as `wordLength` grows at a fixed level, the group is infinite *provided the level is deep enough to separate them*, and if it settles, nothing follows. Verified on the adding machine, which is the trap in its purest form: `AutomatonElementCount[{62, {2, 2}}, 8, level]` gives $4, 8, 16, 17, 17$ for $\text{level} = 2, \ldots, 6$ — the count saturating at $17$ because words of length $\leq 8$ can only reach $17$ elements, in a group that is $\mathbb{Z}$. Read the other way, $G_L \cong \mathbb{Z}/2^L$ and the radius-$2^{L-1}$ ball closes at *every* level, so ball closure certifies nothing at all. `AutomatonGroupFingerprint[automaton, level]` is the same measurement packaged for a sweep, and `AutomatonNucleus` supplies a genuine sufficient condition in the other direction, contraction plus a level-transitive action being enough for infiniteness in the cases the literature covers. The certificate that is missing is a word-problem test: finiteness is r.e. because $G_L \cong G$ can be confirmed by checking finitely many equalities in $G$, and no export decides equality in $G$ — only in $G_L$. |
| **Searched here** | **Nothing, and the paclet as it stands cannot: every ball it computes lives in a fixed level quotient.** `AutomatonElementCount[automaton, n, L]` counts elements of $G_L$ and `AutomatonGroupBall[automaton, r, L]` lists them, so a ball that closes proves only that a finite group is finite — the adding-machine case above is exactly what those symbols would report. The one finiteness fact this repository does own comes from the other end of the scale and from the [triviality entry](#Triviality)'s criterion rather than from any ball: of the $64$ invertible $(2, 2)$ rules, exactly $16$ have every state inactive and so generate the trivial group, and those $16$ are exactly the codes whose level-$6$ ball has one element, verified this session. Everything else the four sweeps computed — the torsion filter, and `AutomatonGroupFingerprint`'s radius-$1$ to radius-$4$ counts at level $5$ or $6$ — is silent about finiteness in both directions. A recipe *can* be written here, unlike the three entries that have none, but it will be a filter: nothing exported certifies finiteness, because nothing exported decides equality in $G$. |
| **References** | **Undecidable for semigroups** [Gillibert2014](References.md#Gillibert2014), by reduction from Wang tiling; **a more general group form undecidable** [DFRW2020](References.md#DFRW2020). **Positive results** [Klimann2013](References.md#Klimann2013) (two-state or two-letter invertible-reversible) and [DRW2024](References.md#DRW2024) (complete automaton semigroups of bounded extended activity). **Open for groups in general** — [Zuk2008](References.md#Zuk2008) and [GNS2000](References.md#GNS2000) state it that way. **Why the paclet's balls cannot certify it** [Nekrashevych2005](References.md#Nekrashevych2005) for the level quotients, with the adding machine of [GrigorchukZuk2001](References.md#GrigorchukZuk2001) as the counterexample to ball closure. **Sufficient conditions in the other direction** [BGN2003](References.md#BGN2003) for level-transitive branch actions. |

### <a id="Order"></a>Order problem

| Field | |
|---|---|
| **Problem** | Given a finite Mealy automaton and a word $w$ in its states, does $w$ have finite order in $\mathrm{Aut}(T_k)$? This is the elementwise form of the torsion question the [Burnside entry](#BurnsideTorsion) asks of a whole group. |
| **Status** | `Undecidable`, and settled twice in the same week of October 2017 after standing as a question of Grigorchuk. Gillibert 2018 constructs, for every Turing machine and initial configuration, an element of an automaton group that has finite order **iff** the machine halts; a universal machine then gives one automaton group with undecidable order problem, and the same group has undecidable Engel problem, answering a question of Bartholdi. Bartholdi–Mitrofanov 2020, posted a day later, prove undecidability **even under the assumption that the group is contracting** — so the structural hypothesis that makes the word problem fast buys nothing here — and in the same paper that the word problem is undecidable for functionally recursive groups. Partial results run the other way, on torsion rather than on decidability: a connected reversible Mealy automaton on $3$ states cannot generate an infinite Burnside group (Klimann–Picantin–Savchuk 2014), extended to any prime number of states by Godin–Klimann 2016. |
| **Automaton translation** | $w$ has finite order iff its level orders $\mathrm{ord}_L(w) = \lvert \rho_L(w) \rvert$ — nondecreasing in $L$, with $\mathrm{ord}(w) = \sup_L \mathrm{ord}_L(w)$ — are eventually constant. In the iterated-automaton reading of the [periodicity entry](#Periodicity), $\mathrm{ord}_L(w)$ is the lcm of the evolution periods over all tapes of length $L$, so finite order says: the evolution is periodic with a single bound serving every tape length. `AutomatonWordOrders[automaton, word, levels]` returns exactly this sequence. |
| **Faithfulness** | `Equivalent`, and the undecidability is what the equivalence costs — the quantifier over $L$ cannot be eliminated. The certificate asymmetry is the **reverse** of the one in the [finiteness entry](#Finiteness), and it inverts what this repository's torsion machinery is doing. Finite order is semi-decidable: if $\mathrm{ord}(w) = n$ then $w^n = 1$, and $w^n = 1$ is a decidable check on a finite-state automorphism ([triviality entry](#Triviality)), so enumerating $n$ terminates on exactly the elements of finite order. Infinite order is therefore **not** semi-decidable — Gillibert's reduction makes halting equivalent to finite order, and if infinite order also had finite certificates the problem would be decidable. **Every element of finite order has a finite certificate; no element of infinite order has one.** |
| **Witness shape** | Finite order: the exponent $n$ together with the section closure of $w^n$, every member inactive. Sections of a length-$\ell$ word are again length-$\ell$ words in the states, so that closure is a reachability search inside a set of at most $s^{\ell}$ words — small in practice, and unbounded in $n$, which is the part no computation can predict. Infinite order: no witness shape in general. What is available is the lower bound $\mathrm{ord}(w) \geq \mathrm{ord}_L(w)$ at any single level, and for named automata a proof: $\mathrm{ord}_L(a) = 2^L$ for the adding machine $a = \sigma(1, a)$, by induction on $L$. |
| **Test recipe** | `AutomatonWordOrders[automaton, word, levels]`, and it is the most-run recipe in the repository — all four sweeps are `AutomatonTorsionFreeCandidateQ[automaton, wordLength, levels]`, which is this call over `AutomatonWordBall[automaton, wordLength]` with the plateau condition applied. The orders are nondecreasing in $L$ and constant from some level on exactly when the word has finite order, so growth between two well-separated levels is evidence of infinite order and a plateau is evidence of finite order. Verified: `AutomatonWordOrders[{5125, {3, 2}}, {1, 2}, {2, 4, 6, 8}]` $= 4, 16, 64, 256$ (Aleshin, growing), against $2, 2, 2$ for the Grigorchuk generators and $8, 16, 16$ for their product at levels $4, 8, 12$ (plateaued). **Both readings are filters, and they fail in opposite ways that the entry's undecidability explains.** A plateau can resume — the lamplighter's generator runs $4, 8, 8, 16, 16$ at levels $2, 4, 6, 8, 10$, which is why `AutomatonTorsionFreeCandidateQ` names it in its own docstring — and growth over finitely many levels is not a proof either. The certificate side, $w^n = 1$, is decidable and unimplemented: it is a word-problem check, and no export performs one. So this entry's recipe is entirely on the side Gillibert's reduction shows has no finite certificates, while the side that *has* certificates is the side the paclet cannot test. |
| **Searched here** | **Every torsion computation in this repository sits on the side of the problem that provably has no certificates.** `AutomatonTorsionFreeCandidateQ[automaton, wordLength, levels]` keeps an automaton when no word of length $\leq$ `wordLength` has had its order plateau between two well-separated levels — evidence of infinite order, which by the field above can never be upgraded to a proof by adding levels. All four sweeps are built on it: $(2, 2)$ $64 \to 8$ strict candidates ($2$ classes), $(3, 2)$ $5832 \to 133$ classes, $(2, 3)$ $231 \to 55$ classes, $(4, 2)$ $22592 \to 5822$ classes, $6018$ in total. The filter's calibration is on record and measures exactly the plateau-and-resume failure the undecidability theorem says cannot be avoided: over $(2, 3)$ the adjacent levels $\{3,4\}, \{4,5\}$ leave $41$ of $231$ classes where the well-separated $\{4,6\}, \{5,7\}$ leave $55$, so $14$ classes plateau early and resume later. The named instance is the lamplighter, $(2, 2)$ code $54$, whose generator has orders $4, 8, 8, 16, 16$ at levels $2, 4, 6, 8, 10$. In the other direction **nothing has been searched at all**: the $5699$ automata the $(3, 2)$ filter discarded, and their counterparts in the other three spaces, were dropped without anyone computing the exponent that would have certified them ([torsion entry](#BurnsideTorsion)). |
| **References** | **Undecidable** [Gillibert2018](References.md#Gillibert2018) — a group element of finite order iff a Turing machine halts, answering a question of Grigorchuk, and undecidable for the Engel problem too — and [BartholdiMitrofanov2020](References.md#BartholdiMitrofanov2020), which keeps it undecidable under contraction and drops the word problem's decidability once finite-stateness goes. **Where the class is small enough to force finite order** [KPS2014](References.md#KPS2014), [GodinKlimann2016](References.md#GodinKlimann2016). **The torsion examples the filter was calibrated on** [Grigorchuk1980](References.md#Grigorchuk1980), [GuptaSidki1983](References.md#GuptaSidki1983), [Aleshin1972](References.md#Aleshin1972), against the plateau-then-resume behaviour of [GrigorchukZuk2001](References.md#GrigorchukZuk2001). **Machinery** [GNS2000](References.md#GNS2000), [Nekrashevych2005](References.md#Nekrashevych2005). |

### <a id="Conjugacy"></a>Conjugacy problem

| Field | |
|---|---|
| **Problem** | Given two words $u, v$ in the states, is there $g \in G$ with $g^{-1} u g = v$? Two neighbouring questions live in the larger groups the automaton sits inside: are $u, v$ conjugate in $\mathrm{Aut}(T_k)$, or in the group $\mathrm{FAut}(T_k)$ of *all* finite-state automorphisms of $T_k$? |
| **Status** | `Undecidable` for automaton groups — Šunić–Ventura 2012 (arXiv 2010) construct self-similar (free abelian)-by-free groups, generated by finite self-similar sets of tree automorphisms, with unsolvable conjugacy problem. The word problem in them is solvable, so this is a genuine separation and not a corollary of some coarser failure. Decidable in the bounded class: Bondarenko–Bondarenko–Sidki–Zapata 2013 (arXiv 2010) prove that under contraction plus finiteness of what they call the *orbit-signalizer*, two finite-state automorphisms are conjugate in $\mathrm{Aut}(T)$ iff they are conjugate in $\mathrm{FAut}(T)$ and that this is decidable, and that both hypotheses hold for bounded automorphisms — giving decidability of the simultaneous conjugacy problem in the group of bounded automata. |
| **Automaton translation** | Conjugacy pushes forward down the whole tower: if $u \sim v$ in $G$ then $\rho_L(u) \sim \rho_L(v)$ in $G_L$, hence in $\mathrm{Sym}(k^L)$, hence $\rho_L(u)$ and $\rho_L(v)$ **have the same cycle type**. Each level therefore supplies one refutation test, and the invariant is already computable from the exports: `AutomatonLevelPermutations[automaton, L]` returns the level action, and its cycle type is the multiset of evolution periods of the [periodicity entry](#Periodicity). |
| **Faithfulness** | `Necessary` — the catalog's first, and chosen against the temptation to promote it. Equal cycle types at every level is implied by conjugacy and implies it at neither of the two steps: level-by-level agreement does not assemble into conjugacy in $\mathrm{Aut}(T_k)$, and conjugacy in $\mathrm{Aut}(T_k)$ does not descend to $G$. The adding machine settles the second gap cheaply: $a$ and $a^{-1}$ have the same cycle type at every level — a single $2^L$-cycle, checked here for $L \leq 5$ — and are conjugate in $\mathrm{Aut}(T_2)$, while $G = \langle a \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}$ is abelian and $a \neq a^{-1}$. A cycle-type test can only ever return *no*. |
| **Witness shape** | To refute: one level and two unequal cycle types — for $a$ against $a^2$ in the adding machine, already at $L = 1$, where $a$ is a transposition and $a^2$ is trivial, and at every level after ($2^L$-cycle against two $2^{L-1}$-cycles). To confirm: an explicit conjugator, a word in the generators and so a finite object, with nothing bounding its length — which is what the undecidability says. In $\mathrm{Aut}(T_k)$ the confirming object is instead the orbit-signalizer, finite exactly in the class where the problem is decidable. |
| **Test recipe** | **The catalog's one `Necessary` recipe, and it is a single line from existing exports.** Conjugate elements of $G$ have conjugate images in every $G_L$, hence equal cycle type on the $k^L$ level vertices, so unequal cycle types at any level is a proof of non-conjugacy and equal types at every level tested proves nothing. Take `perm = AutomatonWordPermutation[automaton, level, word]` and read its cycle type as `Tally` of the cycle lengths — **padded back to $k^{\text{level}}$ points by hand**, because `PermutationCycles` drops fixed points and a census that forgets to pad silently gets the wrong answer (this bit the $(2, 2)$ periodicity census, four rules and one level off). Verified on the Aleshin automaton at level 4: $a$ is a single $16$-cycle, $b$ is two $8$-cycles — different types, so $a \not\sim b$ in $G$, a genuine negative from one permutation each — while $ab$ and $ba$ are both single $16$-cycles, which is the type-agreement conjugate elements are obliged to show and is not evidence of anything. Nothing here does the decidable case: `AutomatonNucleus` identifies contracting codes and boundedness has to be read off `AutomatonWreathRecursion`, but BBSZ's algorithm for bounded automata is not implemented, so even the codes where conjugacy *is* decidable get only the filter. |
| **Searched here** | **Nothing; no conjugacy computation has ever run in this repository and no exported symbol addresses conjugacy.** The ingredient for the necessary test exists and is unused — `AutomatonLevelPermutations` plus one `PermutationCycles`. Two checks were run this session, both on the adding machine (code $62$ in $(2, 2)$) and both illustrating the field above rather than deciding anything: $a$ and $a^{-1}$ share the cycle type $(2^L)$ for $L \leq 5$ while being non-conjugate in $G$, and $a$ against $a^2$ is refuted at every level. The class where the problem is *decidable* is nearly empty in the swept space: exactly $3$ of the $32$ torsion-free $(3, 2)$ codes are bounded automata, and $28$ are provably not ([activity entry](#AmenabilityActivity)). |
| **References** | **Undecidable** [SunicVentura2012](References.md#SunicVentura2012). **Decidable for bounded automata** [BBSZ2013](References.md#BBSZ2013), under contraction plus finiteness of the orbit-signalizer — with [Sidki2000](References.md#Sidki2000) for boundedness and [BN2007](References.md#BN2007) for the contraction it implies. **Where the group-theoretic statement is set up** [GNS2000](References.md#GNS2000), [Nekrashevych2005](References.md#Nekrashevych2005). **The cycle-type filter** needs no citation beyond conjugacy-invariance of cycle type; the `PermutationCycles` fixed-point trap is a Wolfram-specific hazard recorded in the [test recipe](#Conjugacy). |

### <a id="Freeness"></a>Freeness problem

| Field | |
|---|---|
| **Problem** | Given an invertible Mealy automaton on $s$ states, is the group it generates free of rank $s$ on those states? |
| **Status** | `Open` — no decision procedure is known and none is ruled out, which puts it between the [finiteness problem](#Finiteness), where the semigroup case has fallen, and the [order problem](#Order), where the group case has. That automaton groups *can* be free was announced by Aleshin in 1983 for a $3$-state binary automaton and proved by Vorobets–Vorobets 2006. Partial decidability: Klimann 2013 decides freeness for semigroups generated by two-state invertible-reversible automata; D'Angeli–Rodaro 2014 characterise freeness of an automaton group by the boundary dynamics of the enriched dual transducer, and for bireversible automata reduce non-freeness to a finite Schreier orbit in the dual. |
| **Automaton translation** | $G$ is free on the states iff **no nonempty reduced word in the states acts trivially on $T_k$** — the translation is the definition, and the content is that each single instance of it is decidable, being the [triviality entry](#Triviality)'s finite check. Freeness is thus a $\forall$ over an infinite set of individually decidable conditions, which is the same shape as the [order problem](#Order) and, so far, without that problem's undecidability proof. |
| **Faithfulness** | `Equivalent`, with the asymmetry running the same way as in the order entry: **non-freeness is semi-decidable and freeness is not.** Enumerate reduced words, test each for triviality; the search halts exactly when the group fails to be free. A proof of freeness is therefore never the result of a search — Vorobets–Vorobets's is a dynamical argument about the action of the dual automaton, and D'Angeli–Rodaro's characterisation is what replaces the word quantifier by a condition on boundary orbits. |
| **Witness shape** | Non-free: one nonempty reduced word $w$ whose section closure is entirely inactive — a finite certificate, and by the [order entry](#Order)'s remark it lives among the $s^{\lvert w \rvert}$ words of the same length. Free: no finite certificate; the known proofs exhibit a level-transitive action of the dual (semi)group. Scale: the smallest automaton known to generate a nonabelian free group has $s = 3$, $k = 2$ — inside the space this repository has swept most thoroughly. |
| **Test recipe** | Ball counting against the free-group count, and this entry is where it works best — `AutomatonElementCount[automaton, wordLength, level]` compared with $1 + \sum_{j \leq n} 2s\,(2s - 1)^{\,j-1}$, or `AutomatonGroupFingerprint[automaton, level]`'s `"BallGrowth"` compared with the same sequence for $n = 1, \ldots, 4$. A count *below* the free value is a relation and therefore a proof of non-freeness, once the level is deep enough that the collision is not an artifact of the quotient; a count that matches is consistent with freeness and proves nothing, since the level quotients are finite and the free group is not. Verified on the Aleshin automaton, whose ball counts at level 8 are $7, 37, 187$ for radii $1, 2, 3$ against the $F_3$ values $7, 37, 187$ — an exact match, and the same call at level 6 gives $181$ at radius 3, a $3\%$ shortfall that is pure quotient artifact and would read as a relation to anyone who skipped the level check. That is the recipe's one real hazard, and it is the [Milnor](#Milnor) lower-bound problem wearing different clothes. `CanonicalAutomatonCode[automaton]` and `BGKMNSSNumber[automaton]` are what connect a candidate to the literature — the way Aleshin's $a = \sigma(b, c)$, $b = \sigma(c, b)$, $c = (a, a)$ was located inside the swept space as canonical code $5125$ — and the citation, not the computation, is what supplies freeness. |
| **Searched here** | **The one automaton proven to generate a free group is inside the swept $(3, 2)$ space, and this repository has been carrying it without noticing.** The Aleshin automaton $a = \sigma(b, c)$, $b = \sigma(c, b)$, $c = (a, a)$ is code $35610$ in this paclet's encoding, canonical code $5125$, and `BGKMNSSNumber` returns $2240$ — the class the classification identifies with $F_3$. It passes `AutomatonTorsionFreeCandidateQ` at both sweep settings, is one of the $133$ torsion-free candidates of `Data/reps-3-2.wxf`, and sits in the $65$-code **free-like** stratum, which is a ball-count label and not a freeness claim, and which happens here to be right for a reason nothing in the sweep knew. Its ball counts at the sweep's own reference level $6$ are $7, 37, 181, 855$ against the true free-group values $7, 37, 187, 937$; the deficit closes at level $8$, where `AutomatonGroupFingerprint` returns $7, 37, 187, 937$ exactly. That is the [Milnor entry](#Milnor)'s "level counts are lower bounds" as a two-line instance — at the level the sweep used, a free group looks about $9\%$ smaller than it is. **No freeness test has ever run here**, and no group in any swept space has been shown free or non-free by this repository. What the identification does settle for free is Kaplansky on that class: $F_3$ is left-orderable, so $K[F_3]$ is a domain and the empty pass on code $5125$ was a consistency check ([zero-divisor entry](#KaplanskyZeroDivisor)). |
| **References** | **The realization** [Aleshin1983](References.md#Aleshin1983) — no machine identifier exists — with the proof completed in [VorobetsVorobets2006](References.md#VorobetsVorobets2006) and extended in [VorobetsVorobets2010](References.md#VorobetsVorobets2010); [Nekrashevych2008](References.md#Nekrashevych2008) for where free subgroups *cannot* occur, and [Abert2005](References.md#Abert2005) for the closure statement that goes the other way. **Partial decidability** [Klimann2013](References.md#Klimann2013) (two-state invertible-reversible semigroups) and [DAngeliRodaro2014](References.md#DAngeliRodaro2014) (boundary dynamics of the enriched dual). **No algorithm known in general** [Zuk2008](References.md#Zuk2008), [GNS2000](References.md#GNS2000). **Locating a candidate in the swept space** [BGKMNSS2007a](References.md#BGKMNSS2007a), [BGKMNSS2009](References.md#BGKMNSS2009), [BGKMNSS2008](References.md#BGKMNSS2008). |

### <a id="Triviality"></a>Triviality problem

| Field | |
|---|---|
| **Problem** | Given a Mealy automaton, is the group generated by its states trivial? And the finer question underneath it, on which every other entry in this section depends: given a word in the states, does it act trivially on $T_k$ — the word problem. |
| **Status** | `Solved by other means` — both are **decidable**, by an argument old enough to be folklore and written out in GNS 2000. A word $w$ in the states of a finite automaton is itself a finite-state automorphism: its sections form a finite set, computed by closing $\{w\}$ under $\cdot\vert_x$, and $w = 1$ iff every member of that closure is inactive. The group is trivial iff each state is, and since an automaton's state set is already closed under sections, that is one pass over the output column of the transition table. This catalog's closed vocabulary has no token for *decidable* and `Solved by other means` is the nearest fit; the field text carries the rest. |
| **Automaton translation** | The problem is already an automaton statement, which is why the entry is here — it marks where the decidability boundary runs, and the boundary is startlingly close. For one and the same finite input: triviality is linear in the transition table, the word problem is a section-closure search, [finiteness](#Finiteness) is open, [freeness](#Freeness) is open, and [order](#Order) and [conjugacy](#Conjugacy) are undecidable. The hypothesis holding the decidable end up is *finite-stateness* and nothing else — Bartholdi–Mitrofanov 2020 prove the word problem **undecidable** for functionally recursive groups, which are given by the same kind of recursion with the finiteness of the state set dropped. |
| **Faithfulness** | `Equivalent`, and here the token earns its keep, unlike in the [given-automaton amenability entry](#AmenabilityGiven) where the same value labels a tautology that reduces nothing. Both sides are decidable and the automaton side *is* the algorithm. |
| **Witness shape** | Nontrivial: one active state, or for a word, one active member of its section closure — read off the table. Trivial: the whole closure, all inactive. Since the sections of a length-$\ell$ word are again length-$\ell$ words in the states, that closure is a reachability search inside a set of at most $s^{\ell}$ words, so the certificate is finite and explicit at every instance — the only entry in the catalog of which that is true in both directions. |
| **Test recipe** | **The one entry whose recipe is complete in both directions for the group, and incomplete for the word.** For the group: read `AutomatonWreathRecursion[automaton, Method -> "String"]` and check that no state permutes its symbols — one pass over the output column — or equivalently `InvertibleAutomatonQ` together with the level test `AutomatonElementCount[automaton, wordLength, level] == 1`. Verified across the whole smallest space: `Count[Map[Function[code, AutomatonElementCount[{code, {2, 2}}, 3, 6]], InvertibleAutomatonCodes[{2, 2}]], 1]` is $16$, exactly the codes with both states inactive, and exactly the "generate the trivial group" figure the [zero-divisor entry](#KaplanskyZeroDivisor)'s $(2, 2)$ funnel reports — now derived rather than measured, since an invertible $(2, 2)$ automaton has $2 \cdot 4 = 8$ choices per state and $4^2 = 16$ of the $8^2 = 64$ leave both inactive. **For a word there is no recipe, and that single gap is what limits [finiteness](#Finiteness), [order](#Order) and [freeness](#Freeness) to filters.** The pieces are all exported — `AutomatonSectionTable[automaton]`, `AutomatonWordSection[sections, word, x]`, `AutomatonSection[automaton, word, treeWord]` — and the missing function is the closure: start from $\{w\}$, close under $\cdot\vert_x$ until the set repeats, and report whether every member is inactive. It terminates inside a set of at most $s^{\ell}$ words, and it is the one addition that would give three other entries their certificate side. Note that the sections returned are *words*, not reduced group elements, so the closure has to be taken up to the level action or it will not terminate in practice: `AutomatonSection[{62, {2, 2}}, {1}, {0, 0}]` returns `{2}`, the trivial state written as a length-1 word rather than as `{}`. |
| **Searched here** | The criterion is confirmed across the smallest space: of the $64$ invertible $(2, 2)$ rules exactly $16$ have both states inactive, and those $16$ are exactly the codes whose level-$6$ ball has one element, verified this session with `AutomatonElementCount`. That $16$ is the "generate the trivial group" figure the [zero-divisor entry](#KaplanskyZeroDivisor)'s $(2, 2)$ funnel reports, and it is now derived rather than measured — an invertible $(2, 2)$ automaton has $2$ output choices and $4$ section choices per state, so the inactive ones number $4^2 = 16$ out of $8^2 = 64$. Beyond that, **no word-problem certificate has ever been computed in this repository.** Every equality test here is made in a level quotient, which decides equality in $G_L$ and not in $G$, and the section machinery that would decide it — `AutomatonSectionTable`, `AutomatonWordSection`, `AutomatonSection` — is exported but never assembled into a triviality test. That single gap is why the [finiteness](#Finiteness), [order](#Order) and [freeness](#Freeness) entries have filters and no certificates: all three of their finite certificates are word-problem checks. |
| **References** | **Decidability, and the section-closure argument** [GNS2000](References.md#GNS2000), with [Nekrashevych2005](References.md#Nekrashevych2005) for the section vocabulary. **Where the boundary runs** [BartholdiMitrofanov2020](References.md#BartholdiMitrofanov2020): the word problem is undecidable for functionally recursive groups, i.e. the same recursion with finite-stateness dropped — the single hypothesis holding the decidable end up. **What it gates** the certificate side of [finiteness](#Finiteness) ([Gillibert2014](References.md#Gillibert2014), [DFRW2020](References.md#DFRW2020)), [order](#Order) ([Gillibert2018](References.md#Gillibert2018)) and [freeness](#Freeness) ([DAngeliRodaro2014](References.md#DAngeliRodaro2014)). **Complexity, for anyone implementing the closure** [BartholdiMitrofanov2020](References.md#BartholdiMitrofanov2020) and the automaton-semigroup word-problem literature it cites. |

### <a id="Periodicity"></a>Periodicity and its dependence on the initial condition

| Field | |
|---|---|
| **Problem** | The essay's own question, and the only entry in this catalog posed from the automaton side. Iterating a Mealy automaton on a tape of length $L$ from a fixed initial state gives an orbit with a transient $\tau(x)$ and a period $\pi(x)$: when is the evolution periodic, and how do $\tau$ and $\pi$ depend on the initial tape $x$? Translated here *into* group language rather than out of it. |
| **Status** | `Open` as a general question, with the invertible case pinned down. Two halves follow immediately from the identification below and are recorded in the `Periodicity` notebook: an invertible automaton has $\tau(x) = 0$ for every $x$, the one-pass map being a bijection of a finite set, and $\pi(x)$ divides $\mathrm{ord}_L(g)$ for every $x$ by Lagrange. What stays open is everything asymptotic — whether $\pi$ is constant in $x$, how it grows in $L$, and whether level orders can be unbounded without being eventually a power of $k$, which the notebook poses as a Question and this repository has not answered. |
| **Automaton translation** | Running the other way, and it is an identity rather than a translation: **the one-pass map on tapes of length $L$ is the level-$L$ permutation $\rho_L(g)$ of the initial state $g$**, since reading a length-$L$ word and emitting a length-$L$ word *is* the action of $g$ on level $L$ of $T_k$. For invertible automata this gives, at once: $\pi(x)$ is the length of the cycle of $\rho_L(g)$ containing $x$; the multiset $\{\pi(x) : x \in X^L\}$ is the cycle type of $\rho_L(g)$; $\mathrm{ord}_L(g) = \mathrm{lcm}_x \pi(x)$; and $g$ has infinite order iff the periods are unbounded in $L$ ([order entry](#Order)). The dependence question then has a one-line group answer: **the period is the same for every initial condition of length $L$ iff $\langle \rho_L(g) \rangle$ acts freely on the $k^L$ vertices** — iff no nontrivial power of $g$ fixes a level-$L$ vertex, iff $\pi(x) = \mathrm{ord}_L(g)$ for every $x$. |
| **Faithfulness** | `Equivalent`, and this is the only entry in the catalog whose translation is exact **and** finitely checkable at each instance: for a fixed $L$ the free-action condition is a cycle-type computation on $k^L$ points, terminating, with no level ceiling and no plateau caveat. The infinite quantifier is only the one over $L$ — the same one that makes the [order problem](#Order) undecidable — so "independent of the initial condition at every length" inherits that ceiling while every fixed length is decided outright. |
| **Witness shape** | Dependence at length $L$: two tapes with different periods, equivalently a cycle type of $\rho_L(g)$ with two distinct entries — a certificate of size $k^L$ read off one permutation. Independence at every length: a proof, e.g. $\rho_L(a)$ is a single $2^L$-cycle for the adding machine, so the action is regular and $\pi \equiv 2^L$. Note that the object here is a **pointed** automaton: the marked initial state is part of the data, relabelling the states changes the answer while preserving the group, so this is the one entry whose question is not an invariant of $G$. |
| **Test recipe** | **The only recipe in the catalog that decides its question outright at each instance, with no level ceiling and no plateau caveat.** The one-pass map on length-$L$ tapes *is* $\rho_L(g)$, so everything reduces to a cycle type: take `AutomatonLevelPermutations[automaton, level]`, which returns an Association from each state to its permutation of the $k^{\text{level}}$ vertices, index it at the marked state (or use `AutomatonWordPermutation[automaton, level, word]` for a word), and the period from tape $x$ is the length of the cycle containing $x$, $\mathrm{ord}_L(g) = \mathrm{lcm}$ of the cycle lengths, and **the period is independent of the initial condition iff all cycle lengths are equal**, i.e. iff $\langle \rho_L(g) \rangle$ acts freely. `IteratedFiniteAutomatonPeriodicStop[automaton, s0, init, t]` runs the evolution itself and stops at the first repeated row, which is the direct check that the identification is right — on `{54, {2, 2}}` from tape $\{1, 1, 0, 1\}$ it returns 9 rows. Verified as a full census, with `uniformQ[perm]` the one-line predicate "the padded cycle type of `perm` has a single distinct value": `Count[InvertibleAutomatonCodes[{2, 2}], code_ /; AllTrue[Range[2, 8], Function[level, uniformQ[AutomatonLevelPermutations[{code, {2, 2}}, level][1]]]]]` is $56$, so $56$ of the $64$ rules are independent at every $L \leq 8$ and $8$ are not. **The one trap, and it is easy to hit:** `PermutationCycles` drops fixed points, so the cycle type must be padded back to $k^L$ entries by hand — without the padding the same census returns $60$ and puts the first failure at level 3 instead of level 2. With it, `{54, {2, 2}}` at $L = 4$ pointed at its inactive generator gives type $(1, 1, 2, 4, 4, 4)$ — three distinct periods coexisting, the lamplighter again — against the adding machine's single $16$-cycle, period $2^L$ from every tape. |
| **Searched here** | The full census over the smallest space, computed this session. Of the $64$ invertible $(2, 2)$ rules, **$56$ have period independent of the initial condition at every level $L \leq 8$, and exactly $8$ do not** — all $8$ failing already at $L = 2$. The failures are not spread over the space: in every one of them the *marked* state is inactive, which is necessary and far from sufficient, since $32$ of the $64$ rules have an inactive marked state; and they fall into four relabelling classes, $22$, $54$, $60$ and $97$, each occurring twice. **Two of the four are the lamplighter**, canonical codes $54$ and $60$, pointed at the inactive generator: cycle type $(1, 1, 2, 4, 4, 4)$ at $L = 4$, so periods $1$, $2$ and $4$ coexist, the two fixed tapes being $1^{L-1}0$ and $1^L$, and level orders $1, 2, 4, 4, 8, 8$ for $L \leq 6$ — unbounded, so the element has infinite order. That is the [Atiyah entry](#Atiyah)'s automaton, and this is the one property in the catalog that separates it from the adding machine (code $62$), whose action is *regular*: one $2^L$-cycle, period $2^L$ from every tape. The other two classes, $22$ and $97$, have level orders constant at $2$ — the finite-order case, with periods $1$ and $2$ coexisting. The identification $\pi(x) = $ cycle length was verified against `IteratedFiniteAutomatonPeriodicStop` on all $64$ rules at $L = 4$. Nothing has been computed in $(3, 2)$, $(2, 3)$ or $(4, 2)$, and the notebook's three conjectures — the adding machine's period $2^L$ (to $L = 9$), the lamplighter's level order $2^{\lceil \log_2 (L+1) \rceil}$ (to $L = 16$), and the maximal-period codes $52, 62, 148, 158$ (for $4 \leq L \leq 10$) — remain conjectures. |
